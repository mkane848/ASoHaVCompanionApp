# Game Overview

**A Story of Heroes and Villains (ASoHaV) is a narrative fantasy game with high-stakes, goal-driven combat.**

This game gives you the tools to craft and tell a story in a world only your table can. You and your fellow players are both author and audience for your favorite High Fantasy movie or television epic. The rules exist to support your story, there is no need to thumb through them to figure out how to take an action; to do it, do it. When you need to roll dice, the sheet in front of you will tell you how the result affects you or the world around you.

Your Heroes will be disparate, complicated people who come together and embark on a journey that will see them change their world in some fundamental way \- big or small. Along their path, they’ll discover their true selves as well as a chosen family they’ll come to defend until the end of days. 

On that path, your Heroes will see fantastic places, meet kind weirdos and cruel monsters; encounter mysteries never before solved; protect the defenseless; they’ll be bold; they’ll take risks; they’ll sing by the fire with friends; they’ll nurture bonds with deep roots; they’ll take down tyrants and trolls alike; they’ll confront the things they’ve always run from (and not wait 30 minutes to swing your sword at a bag of hit points). 

A Story of Heroes and Villains is a game explicitly about Heroes who do all of that. From their first chapter they begin down a path to figuring out the world, themselves, and each other. And should you survive the darkest of evils by relying on those at your side, you’ll have found the place you belong and the people you belong with.

# What is A Story of Heroes and Villains? 

This is a Tabletop Roleplaying game where you and a group of friends tell a story as if you were writing your favorite Epic Fantasy Movie or Television show. You will all take on the responsibilities of crafting the world and the characters within it. Playing the game is largely a conversation, with some structure (the rules) and chaos (the dice) to help drive things forward and create new and interesting situations. 

The great thing about this game is that you are telling this story for and to each other. There’s no better feeling than when something you have all been building toward comes together for the perfect bit of payoff \- either in victory or in tragedy. You all have a pen, you all have a voice. Everyone at the table shares their own ideas that work together to push the story forward. The rules and the dice are here to help guide you \- to surprise you and excite you \- they help set up the next beat of drama for you to explore.

Most of you will be Heroes, directly in control of one of the main characters of your story. One of you will be the Game Master, directly in control of the world around your Heroes. Together, you'll bring your own world to life in a way only your group can. This is your story. Tell it well. Tell it together. Have fun. 

# What Defines This Game?

This game sprung forth from 20 years of playing TTRPGs and consuming Fantasy stories alongside them. In that time we’ve discovered a lot of game mechanics and narrative flavor that really work for our groups and a lot that don’t. What lies before you is the distillation of alllll of that. 

So, is this game for you? Well, are you and your group: 

* Traditional fantasy players who want more character-driven, narrative play.  
* Narrative-game players who want more consequential, decision-rich combat.  
* Looking for a game that avoids excessive crunch without making danger feel toothless while giving you the tools to tell a meaningful story. 

If you said yes to any one of these (and also you like elves and dragons and magic) then yes\! This is a game for you. Read on. 

**Fiction-First storytelling.**   
The rules exist to support the narrative, not the other way around. No mechanical actions happen without the story driving the reason for a rule. You want to Play to Find Out. Nobody at the table knows exactly what is going to happen and when mechanics are triggered, they will never dictate a result of “oh you don’t get through that door, try something else.” They’ll completely change the stakes on everyone at the table\! Maybe you trip a magical alarm or your biggest rival happens to see you fiddling at the lock. The game is never static, the story is always finding new ways to intrigue you both as players and audience members. 

A different way to phrase that is \- “if you say it, do it.” Rather than hunting for what rule can allow you to do something in the game, just do the thing you want to do and the rules will be there to tell you what happens after you do. 

**Fast, high stakes, goal oriented combat.**   
Much of what you find in this game could be labeled as being designed within the Powered By the Apocalypse design philosophy. It draws inspiration from the design framework first developed by Vincent Baker for his 2010 game Apocalypse World, and the many, many wonderful games in the PbtA lineage thereafter. However,  there is one big part of this game where we move away from that a bit: Combat. 

The point of “combat” in most narrative heavy games is to function like any other scene in a movie or television show. And as such there is no mechanical distinction between “Role Play” scenes and “Combat” scenes. But the design exploration of what combat can look like has changed drastically since 2010 and we believe this game has found a way to marry Fiction-First, PbtA design with a fully fleshed out, high-stakes combat system that respects your time.

In a game of High Fantasy you’re going to be fighting evil and slaying monsters\! And I think sometimes, in heavy narrative games, it can feel like the tools your Heroes use to navigate social situations are the same ones they use to solve violent ones. In this game, you are making tactical moves in Combat encounters that can dynamically change the stakes of your story. Combat is fast, dangerous, and still finds a way to be easily digestable, while giving back the tactical choices you may miss when playing other narrative-driven games; all without ever wasting your time feeling like you “just have to get through this round” for the good stuff to happen. 

**The game is yours**  
And so, before we really get started I want to say, once this book (PDF??) is in your hands, it’s *yours*. Not ours. We’re not in charge of how you have your fun. The whole book is merely our best suggestion of how to have a great, frictionless time telling your Epic Fantasy Story. When in doubt, do what feels most fun. 

# The Setting

The setting is, not to sound like a broken record, High Fantasy. It is built around the idea that the heroes will journey through both dangerous wilds and glorious roads; that they will visit small hamlets in a marsh and enormous port cities on the coast; that they will face down foes in the realm of politics and by way of violence. There will be magic\! There will be monsters\! There will be manipulation\! 

But this text does not contain a codified “setting.” In fact, it is quite the opposite. As part of starting a game of ASoHaV, in addition to creating your characters for your adventuring party, everyone at the table will take part in crafting your unique world. 

# The Players

This game has one player who will be the Game Master (GM) and between two and five players who will be characters. 

For the players portraying characters, it is their responsibility to embody their character’s core conflicts and strengths. They are tasked with bringing the main characters of your tale to life. At all times, you as players should engage with the premise you’ve all agreed to dive into and drive the story forward in the way only your character can. Take risks; protect others; defeat the bad guys. In short \- be heroes\! 

The Game Master is responsible for bringing the world around the main characters to life; to make it feel lived in, dangerous, and wonderous. They play all of your side characters, good and evil, and work together with the players to shine the spotlight in the most interesting places. 

Neither role \- Game Master or character \- is solely responsible for “the story.” In AsoHaV you are all playing to find out what happens next. The GM may set the stage and present interesting opportunities, but that is derived from the histories of all of the characters and the world you set up together. The rules and moves at everyone’s disposal give characters and GMs alike the power to control the narrative at some points and be dragged down the stream of where it takes you at others. Part of the fun is discovering it together\!

In fact, should you ever feel the push to plan what “should” happen next, I implore you not to. There is a real magic to letting the characters and the world find ways to surprise you. The mechanics in this game exist to help you reveal every layer of the proverbial artichoke, and what you’ll find at the end will be like nothing you could have planned for. I don’t want you to cheat yourself out of the butterflies you’ll get when it all comes together as if you planned it, but it is a surprise to everyone at the table. Your characters don’t know where they are headed, so don’t always decide for them. Once you set them and the world up and push over the first domino, it’ll all work out. And soon, before your very eyes, those characters and the world around them will come to life. 

# Getting Started

A Story of Heroes and Villains plays as if you were writing and witnessing your favorite Fantasy movie, TV show, or book. You get to live the most interesting stuff and the boring moments live on the cutting room floor (what interesting means is totally up to your group\!). You do whatever makes sense for your character or, if you’re the GM, the world around the characters, and the mechanics respond to what you do to help you tell a story that is always driving toward interesting moments. 

Of course, as a TTRPG, you’re going to be rolling dice. Always a D6, mostly 2d6 plus a stat, but sometimes 1D6\! However, explicitly, the dice aren’t telling you whether you succeed or fail. They’re telling you what happens next.

## Live and Play Within the Fiction

The game isn’t structured so that (outside of combat) you’ll say “I use this move.” Instead, you tell or show your group what your character does. If you want to see if there’s a secret passage, don’t ask to make a roll to find out, simply say something like “I start pulling books off the shelf, checking behind the case, looking for anything out of place.” That action might trigger a move. If it does, you roll. If it doesn’t, it just happens. Sometimes the game is an unbroken conversation without a roll. 

A roll only comes into play when there is risk of interesting consequences (positive or negative) at play. If the outcome of something is unclear and/or the stakes matter, you roll. If the outcome is simple or obvious, it just happens. 

The dice come out when things could go wrong or go interesting. 

To put it simply, To do it, you do it.

## The Dice

When a move triggers, (for example, the Assess the Situation move triggers when you assess the situation to figure out what is going on in a moment of risk or conflict), you roll 2d6 plus one of your character’s Virtue (the move will tell you which). The result on the die sets the stakes for the moment at hand\! 

* **10+**,  You get what you want, sometimes with something extra  
* **7–9**, You get what you want, but there’s a cost, complication, or twist  
* **6-**, You likely don’t get the outcome you wanted, and something changes \- often for the worse. However, you learn from your failures and will always “mark Potential” on a 6-. You can trade in 5 Potential when your party rests to level up\! 

Notice that on a “miss” something is going to change. The story is moving forward no matter what you roll. You never just fail and hit a wall. You won’t roll poorly and get stuck staring at a locked door. When you miss, the situation changes. Maybe a guard spots you or a trap triggers; maybe you DO get what you wanted, but it creates a bigger problem. 

What that is comes out in the conversation between the GM and the other players. 

## The Game is a Conversation

You’re going to be talking. To each other. A lot. The most basic game loop looks like this: 

* The GM describes a situation.  
* The characters respond by doing or saying whatever makes sense.  
* A move might trigger or something might simply happen.   
* The stakes change and the GM continues the conversation by delivering that new information to the characters, either by way of a Non-Player Character (NPC) or by describing the world around them. 

And then it keeps going. Back and forth, over and over. And along the way, really interesting stuff will happen and before you know it you’ll be entrenched in a story that you are all writing and performing for each other. That’s the game\! You can do it\! 

## Overview of the Gameplay

I’ve mentioned that a session plays like an episode of a show or a chapter in a book. In a session, you can probably expect to participate in one or two big, memorable moments \- maybe a venture through the Whispering Wood or adventuring through the Shadowing Mines; maybe you’re negotiating with nobles or taking down a horde of Trolls. Whatever those may be, they’ll be connected by smaller scenes that connect it all together. Those could be meeting new allies, making camp in a dangerous marsh, or planning your attack on the Keep of Garadale. 

Each moment is driven by character choices. Your Playbooks (the sheet with your character details on it) will have all the juicy drama needed to serve you up the conflict that will lead to these moments. As a Party you’ll also have a big goal. Maybe that goal will be “save the blacksmith’s daughter from the goblins” or “retrieve the Scroll of M’Guffin.” You might resolve a full goal in one session. You might not. Both are right\!.There’s no strict pacing requirement laid out in the rules. The only thing that matters is that you’re doing interesting things to you and your characters.

### The Adventuring Party

On your Playbook sheet, you’ll see a place for each character’s goals and internal conflicts (DEFINE). In addition, your group shares a larger goal. What that is will change, sometimes often, sometimes infrequently. The individual goals and party goals will first spring forth from character creation. When you make your character’s and adventuring party, you’ll be presented with questions and prompts that build those tensions naturally.

After those goals are out in the open, the GM and other players are free to use that material as it feels natural. You don’t need to force anything \- it will show up in play.

### The Scenes Make the Story

Think of moments in your game like scenes in a movie. A scene is typically one location or one situation where your characters come into conflict with *something.* The opposing force might be another character or NPC, a societal system, a natural event or creature, or a magical or technological mystery. The conflicting forces can be as small of a scale as a rickety bridge across a river or as large as a final confrontation with the evil necromancer who has been behind all of the party’s woes for months. 

Conflict is what drives the story. Without it, you run the risk of the characters you are portraying saying, “Yeah, so? Why should I care?” And that just takes the air out. 

When you (as a GM or otherwise) are framing a scene to play out, keep in mind what you’d like the central conflict of this moment to be. What is going to stand in the way of the party? And once that conflict has played to its natural conclusion, the scene is over\! You don’t need to play out every single moment. Start late, get out early.  Open the scene at the last possible moment, starting after anything that wouldn’t be enjoyable to play, and ending it as soon as the main point is made. 

Or, to put it simply, skip the boring parts.

### Framing a Scene? 

Often, it will be the GM who is framing the next scene (though they can ask players to do this as well). They’ll describe what’s happening \- evoking what the characters see, hear, smell, etc; frame the conflict at hand, and then ask: “What do you do?” And then the players act, react. They make decisions. And from those decisions, the stakes of the scene will change and evolve. 

When starting a scene, sometimes the GM will start players in the middle of the action. “You stand hidden at the edge of the forest clearing, the Tomb of the Barrow King, guarded by a pack of six-legged beasts, looms in the misty distance. What do you do?” 

This scene may come after the party decides to travel to the Tomb. If there isn’t inherent risk in playing out the travel, don’t. Maybe you frame it as a passive scene, the GM describing the travel and ending the description as the players “arrive” at the Tomb.

Framing a scene means you’re opening the curtain on something that matters to the Heroes, and then you all deal with it, passing the spotlight between one another to play out the conflict. 

I called out, when starting a new scene, that you want to "start late and leave early." All this means is that a scene should begin as close to the central conflict as possible and end at the exact moment its dramatic purpose is achieved. If you can cut out unnecessary entrances, polite pleasantries, and lingering exits, the table can maintain a tight, engaging pace that respects their own time and your feelings of engagement. The more you do this, the more you can stay focused on what matters \- the vital action and emotional beats that drive your story forward.

In Combat, this is explicitly defined to the table. “You are victorious in this Combat when you save the town crier/retrieve the holy relic.” Outside of Combat, this framing is more implicit. As a GM, it is your job to listen to the characters and the NPCs and, when you see the climax of a scene has happened \- “two characters reach an agreement; an NPC reveals a dramatic secret, an NPC flees the premises” \- it is okay to “cut.” This is really a vibes thing, you’ll get better as you play. When you feel all that needs to happen in a scene has happened, jump to the next, most interesting thing and frame the new situation.

### The Spotlight

At any given time, the game is focused on someone. That’s the spotlight. It is like the camera is pointing right at them. It might be one of the players or one of the NPCs. The character with the spotlight is taking action \- whatever that means to them. As good collaborators, you want to share the spotlight to highlight your fellow players at the table. Players will often pass the spotlight by having their character ask questions or pose problems to other characters. At any point, the GM can move the spotlight from one character to another by “picking up” the light or camera and “pointing it” at another character, cutting to a new moment or giving a character a chance to respond. 

Keeping the spotlight moving gives everyone a chance to be highlighted and also a chance to support and engage with their fellow player’s choices. What you do when you have the spotlight is up to you. You can describe what your character does or you can speak directly as them. Whatever method, you’re making decisions that will drive your shared story (and may trigger moves\!) forward. 

### Combat Within Fiction

In ASoHaV, Combat is a different mode of play from everything described before (also known as Freeplay). 

You enter combat when the scene requires violence and that violence has a meaningful outcome, high stakes, or if failing your violent goal would change the story or characters. If a fight feels more like an obstacle, it’s resolved quickly with a single roll or just played out in the conversation.

At the table you start Combat when you have a goal that requires violence. You’re often not just fighting to “win.” Every combat has a purpose. Perhaps the Parry is rescuing an ally, escaping a treacherous jailer, stealing a precious artifact from a crime lord, or stopping a titan from reaching a defenseless hamlet. Combat isn’t asking the characters to defeat every enemy on the map but rather it is asking them to complete a specific objective. 

#### How Combat Plays

Combat in A Story of Heroes and Villains is played on a map with spaces (either hexagonal or square). You still are driving the fiction forward, but Combat “zooms in” and shows us more of the moment-to-moment minutia. You’re making tactical decisions, considering your position, turn order, and team coordination. But you’re still playing in the fiction. You’re never just saying “I attack.” You’re describing what you do through meaningful choices and moves trigger as a result.

Dice rolls in combat follow the same rules as in Freeplay. There is no pass/fail \- every roll moves you closer or farther from your goal. Good rolls will get you closer to your combat goal and bad roles may progress things only a little, or with negative consequences. No matter what, your turn will change the situation at hand. 

Combat ends when the goal is achieved. If your party has indeed grabbed the artifact, freed the prisoner, or escaped the danger you don’t need to finish the fight by taking out all the remaining bad guys. Once the goal is satisfied, the rest plays out in the conversation. 

You cut to what happens next.

## Have Fun

And that’s the basics\! You frame a scene, the GM asks the players what they do, everyone acts within the fiction and the mechanics outlined in this text will respond. Then you can watch as conflict sparks and the story changes. There’s no need for some big plan, you’re all working together and will find out what happens together. At the end of each session you’ll end up with what might be your next favorite fantasy epic. 

I also want to stress, if anything in the book doesn’t work for you, or if you find a better way to play \- do that. These mechanics are here to help spark interesting moments. Play the way that works best for you. Don’t get caught up in the specifics. You’re not beholden to me\! I don’t choose what is fun for your group, you do. 

So go have fun. 

# The Game Structure

Okay so you’re reading this and I’ve used all of this flowery language to talk about what it “feels” like to play this game. Great. I’ve outlined some of the basics previously. But if you find yourself asking “okay but what does a session look like? What am I actually doing in a session?” Awesome. Check it out.

A Story of Heroes and Villains has two basic modes of play \- Freeplay and Combat. 

You go into Combat when the moment at hand requires violence and the outcome of that violence is meaningful to the players; the stakes are high and failing your Combat goal would change the story or characters in some way. Combat is turn-based and “zooms in” to the moment-to moment action; every little choice moves you closer or farther from your Combat Goal. If a fight feels more like an obstacle, it’s resolved quickly with a single roll or just played out in Freeplay. 

Freeplay is everything that isn’t combat. There is no turn structure. Often, Freeplay begins with the GM describing a situation. Then, the characters respond by doing or saying whatever makes sense. Then, a move might trigger or something might simply happen. After that, the stakes change and the GM continues the conversation by delivering that new information to the characters, either by way of a Non-Player Character (NPC) or by describing the world around them. 

And then it keeps going. Back and forth, over and over. And along the way, really interesting stuff will happen and before you know it you’ll be entrenched in a story that you are all writing and performing for each other. That’s the game\! You can do it\! There are other bits and pieces to help you structure both Combat and Freeplay, but that’s for later in this text.

# Motifs (Maybe called Aspects?)

**Motifs** are the core aspects of your Hero. They help define their background, skills, and experiences thus far in their life.

Choose Three Motifs (repeat picks allowed) and write them at the top of each of your Motif Slots. You may change the name of any of the Motifs to better match your character’s flavor. These Motifs will act as buckets for associated Skills, Weaknesses, and Quests. 

If some or none of the Motifs feel right for you, create one or more of your own. 

**Artisan**: You are skilled in a grounded craft or lifelong trade, honed through quiet dedication and practice

**Bearer**: You carry a relic of profound power, it has a legacy of magic, mystery, or myth.

**Devoted**: You have an unshakable loyalty to a cause, creed, or companion.

**Exalted/Lowly**: Your rank, reputation, and influence within the intricate webs of society.

**Exemplar**: You have a defining temperament or worldview. Or perhaps some specific physical, mental, or emotional qualities.

**Fated**: You walk a prophesied path, divine calling, or inexorable fate.

**Forged**: You have been defined by some crucible of your past; a defining trauma, triumph, or event that shaped you.

**Inheritor**: You are greatly affected by the bloodline, culture, and traditions of the people you call your own.

**Mystic**: You wield an ability with the unseen or supernatural forces that shape the world.

**Mythic**: You are a creature of magic or myth.

**Sage**: You carry rare or secret knowledge. Perhaps due to arcane or divine insight, or specialized schooling.

**Sworn**: You’ve sworn a solemn oath, sacred mission, or heavy mantle.

**Virtuoso**: You display a breathtaking raw talent, hard-won skill, or impressive mastery.

## Skill Tags

Skill Tags mechanically express the experiences and proficiencies your character has gained up to this point (you’ll gain more Skill Tags as you adventure). Skill Tags each give \+1 to any relevant rolls. There is no defined list of skills, they are simply words or phrases that say something true and have an effect on how you interact with the world. 

Under each Motif, write one Skill Tag. Below are some suggestions to use. You aren’t confined to using them as they are listed (or at all). If the Skill Tag “Alchemist’s Satchel” feels better for you under “Artisan” than “Bearer,” do that. You can also create any Skill Tag yourself to go under your Motif that you and your GM agree on. 

During the game, when one of your skills applies to any roll you are about to make, you can use the tag to demonstrate your proficiency. Before rolling you can declare your Skill Tag to add its \+1 to the roll. If more than one of your skills applies, you may **Push Yourself.**  Mark one **Condition** and add an additional \+1 to your roll.

## Quests

Quests are what drive your Hero to move forward into dangerous places. Quests create goals for them that will lead them into adventure and conflict \- therefore forcing them to grow and change. As you complete Quests, your Hero will grow stronger and more capable. 

Looking holistically at each Motif with their Skill and Flaw, Write one Quest for each. Quests only need to be a short sentence; ie, “Bring the Ring to the Forge of Doom” “Prove My Worth to My Family” “Find the Sacred Sword” “Free My Sister from Her Captors” “Spread the Word of My Church” “Discover Who Created My Shield” 

Use these varieties to help you define your starting Quests.   
**Dream**: A strongly desired goal or purpose.  
**Tenet**: A principle, belief, truth you want to uphold.   
**Safeguard**: A person, place, or thing you want to preserve or protect.   
**Fascination**: An answer you seek above all else.

# Hero, Party, and Inter-Hero Advancement

## Motif Advancement \- Potential

## Your character gets stronger, individually as they adventure. Certain moves, missing any non-Combat roll, Completing, and Forsaking Quests, and any success or failure where your Flaw Tags are relevant, will have you mark **Potential** on any or a specific Motif. To take an advancement on a Motif, you need to mark potential five times. When you mark your fifth potential, clear your Potential Track and choose one option.

* Add a Skill Tag for this Motif.   
* Add or Remove a Flaw Tag for this Motif (remember, Flaw Tags can help you gain Potential).   
* Gain a Hero Improvement. You can only get a Starting Improvement on any Improvement Tree or an Improvement connected by a line to another Improvement you already have on that same tree. Each can only be chosen once. 

Then you may rewrite or update any one of your Skill or Flaw Tags for this Motif to better reflect your Hero as they are now. 

## Motif Advancement \- Quests

Below each Quest are three Act Breaks and three Foresakes. Whenever your Hero achieves a major success in the pursuit of their Quest, mark an Act Break. Each Act Break must be greater or more significant than the last, building on each other. Whenever your Hero makes a choice that turns away from their Quest, mark a Forsake. Each Forsake must be greater or more significant than the last, building on each other. 

If the narrative introduces an event that would end a Quest (either by completing it or forsaking it) immediately, feel free to mark more than one Act Break or Forsake at once. 

When you complete all the Act Breaks in a Quest, the Quest is complete. Then you may do any of: 

* Mark Potential on that Quest’s Motif until you have 5, then instantly Advance/Level Up that Motif.   
* Re-title this Motif to reflect your journey so far.   
* Edit and revise the text of your Skill and Flaw Tags to reflect how you have changed.   
* Revise or write a new Quest to fit where you are now. 

When you mark your third Forsake in a Quest, the Quest has been abandoned. Then do all of: 

* Re-title this Motif to reflect your journey so far.   
* Remove all of your Skill and Flaw Tags.   
* Write one new Skill and one new Flaw Tag that reflect the new Motif.   
* Write a new Quest to fit this new Motif.   
* Add Potential equal to the total number of Forsakes and Act Breaks to the new Motif, taking a Motif Advance if you mark 5 Potential. 

## Party Advancement \- Rapport

Your party also gets stronger as you adventure together by gaining **Rapport.** To gain an advancement on your Party’s Motif, you need to mark **Rapport** five times. When you mark your fifth rapport, clear your Rapport Track and choose one unique option

* Add a Skill Tag for your Party Motif.    
* Add or Remove a Weakness Tag for your Party Motif (Weakness Tags can help you gain Potential).   
* Gain a Party Improvement. You can only get a Starting Improvement on any Improvement Tree or an Improvement connected by a line to another Improvement you already have on that same tree. Each can only be chosen once. 

In addition to Advancing your Party for long term benefits, Rapport may be spent one at a time for **Aid**. 

**Aid**   
Anyone in the Party can Aid another Hero by removing one Rapport from the Rapport Track to give any other party member \+1 on any roll, even after the dice are rolled. In order to spend Rapport this way, the spender needs to truly be able to help. Describe how you do so in fiction before giving the \+1. 

Any and all party members can spend Rapport from the Track to help on that same roll. Each teammate can only spend one Rapport themselves. Using Rapport this way can take a total miss to a complete success\! 

When you Enter Combat (this means the Combat system, not just some pithy minions you resolve in a roll) against as an adventuring party, add one Rapport to your track. 

* • If everyone has the same end goal for the fight, add another Rapport.  
* • If your party is ill-prepared or off-balance, remove a Rapport.

You mark Rapport at the end of each session when you take the **End the Session** move.  

Whether used for Advancement or Aid, Rapport is used to grow stronger as an adventuring party. It represents your connection and coordination. The more Rapport you gain, the better off you’ll be at taking on new and more powerful challenges\!

# Creating the World

The world you play in at your table might be as small as a few towns and their connecting environments or as large as multiple planes navigable only by the stuff of stars. In order to figure out where your story is going to fit on that scale, your group needs to have a conversation to set expectations. Things will run smoothest when everyone is on the same page. And how do you do that? CATS, of course\! 

CATS stands for Concept, Aim, Tone, Subject Matter. It is a concept from the creative mind of Patrick O'Leary. Following the CATS method will allow your group to hit four essential topics quickly and easily. Just start from the top and make your way down.

**Concept**  
Discuss the game. At a high-level, what’s it about? In this case, this is a Tabletop Roleplaying game where you and a group of friends tell a story as if you were writing your favorite Epic Fantasy Movie or Television show. You will all take on the responsibilities of crafting the world and the characters within it. Playing the game is largely a conversation, with some structure (the rules) and chaos (the dice) to help drive things forward and create new and interesting situations. 

**Aim**  
Explain what the players are trying to accomplish. Are you trying to tell a specific type of story?

* A crew of magical sailors circumnavigating the world in an effort to bring freedom to the oppressed.   
* A party of wanna-be-heroes in a city filled with corrupt aristocrats, uncovering secrets and righting wrongs.   
* A group of heroes from different walks of life journeying across the kingdom in search of an artifact.   
* A team of those who have been hurt by a megalomaniac Dragon, adventuring together to bring it to justice. 

Talk over a few options with your group. What seems fun? What scale are you starting at? Are you aiming for something that lasts five sessions? Ten? Fifty? Get everyone on the same page. 

**Tone**  
Talk about what primary attitude, mood, or flavor your game will take on. What is the default? What touchtones are you drawing from? (Serious vs. Silly, Action vs. Drama, etc.). Come to a consensus on what the group wants.

**Subject Matter**  
Explain what ideas might be explored during gameplay. Do they make anyone uncomfortable? Discuss what boundaries need to be set, if any.

Afterwards, everyone should have the same expectations for the upcoming game. This discussion shouldn’t be long, but it is essential. 

## Character Creation

After you’ve done this, go ahead and create your characters. Going through that process will give you a great jumping off point for what your immediate world looks like. You’ll learn a lot of information about what is known (and not known) to the characters and you can start to shape what kind of world they exist in. 

During character and world creation (and honestly, during the main game itself), remember that you are a collaborative team of writers and directors creating an experience together. Don’t worry about keeping secrets from the other players about a hidden backstory. There should be a distinction between player knowledge and character knowledge and you want to use that to your advantage here. 

Make good use of what you know as players and trust that knowing different things from your characters for the sake of dramatic irony will make for engaging storytelling. On the flip side, trust that your characters know more than you do about the world. You, the player, don’t need to know everything. When it makes sense for you to, you will. 

Once you get into the main gameplay loop you’ll find that the more open you can be with your fellow players, the more you can all direct the story in ways that surprise and delight everyone at the table. 

## Making Your World

Okay, you’ve set expectations, you’ve developed the main characters and their histories with each other. Now it is time to place them in a setting. Throughout character creation, you should have gotten some really juicy ideas about what the world around your characters might be like already. And here’s the fun thing \- you don’t need to know everything right now\! We just need enough to know where we will start in the first session and we’ll sprinkle in some intrigue for future story beats. 

Grab a piece of paper or a shared document, you can do this as a big list, a spreadsheet, or a map you draw together\! Do whatever works for the group. 

This method is adapted from the method found in Lampblack & Brimstone’s *The Perilous Wilds.* 

## Step 1: Where the Adventure Begins

The first thing you’ll want to do in creating your world is to establish what the general landscape looks like around where your adventure will begin. This can be as simple as deciding “how about we’re in some sort of, like, village in between two mountain ranges?” or “what if the characters all start on a small island.” 

Whatever it is, once everyone is in agreement, everyone introduces one detail about the local area and jots it down (or sketches it on the map). Something like a mysterious lighthouse, a thick forest, an old ruin, or a large lake. 

Now, decide on a name for this place. If it is a town, you might call it something like Kaimora, Hillshire, or Red Sun City; if it is an old tomb in a dark wood you might call it something like The Tomb of the Barrow King or simply The Forgotten Mausoleum. 

Next, starting with whoever has an idea first, take turns answering the following questions: 

* This place is famous for:   
* It is infamous for:   
* When it comes to resources (natural or economic), this place is  
  * Prospering, floundering, etc.   
* Because of the resource situation one thing that is happening here is:   
* A notable organization or group that is known to call this place home is:   
* The nearest neighboring town is:   
* That town’s relationship with this place is  
  * Amicable, competitive, envious, charitable, etc. 

Each player then shares one rumor about the place where the party will start. The rumor can be anything\! A bit of everyday drama or something supernatural. It could be a small secret or something everyone here has heard of. 

You’ll jot these down and the GM will use the details of character and world creation to help shape the story as you play. These rumors might appear in strange and twisted ways as the narrative moves forward, and some of them may not come up at all \- but getting them all out there will give you all a great foundation to start your adventure. 

## Step 2: The Surrounding Regions

You know where you’re starting and some top-level details about what that place is. Now let’s zoom out just a bit and put that region in context of the world around it. 

Starting with anyone who wants to speak and then rotating clockwise (including the GM), everyone is going to add a region to the map. A region is just a big section of land or water. You can define what the terrain looks like (a misty forest, rocky plateau, stormy badlands, etc) or by who occupies it (an imperial kingdom, disparate nomads, wild creatures, etc). 

When it’s your turn, say which category of region you are adding (terrain type or political occupant) and then jot that info down. Feel free to give one interesting truth or rumor about that region, but don’t feel pressure to. After that, give the region a name. If you can’t think of a name? Just roll on the table below\! 

The first region should overlap with your starting place. After that, the region you create on your turn can be close or far from your starting region. This part of collaborative world building will codify your starting scale. Are we looking at the coast of one country or kingdom? The entirety of the seas? Or perhaps the magical links between entire realms? 

Whatever type of region you choose, I’d suggest creating something different from the person before you \- the more variety the better. It shouldn’t be a land of *only* witchy forests (unless…). 

Once everyone has gone once, decide if you have enough or if everyone should go again. 

TABLE\! Combine a bunch of region generating tables. Probably should do a town/city one too. The Perilous Wilds is a good starting point. 

## Step 2: Places of Interset

Now, starting with the player whose character is the most traveled, and continuing clockwise, each player adds either an area, a settlement, or a landmark. These are specific places of interest, near or far, you’ll find in the various regions (including your starting region) that may affect the story or your party may visit. Keep making Places of Interest until you feel satisfied (maybe somewhere around eight?). 

The three categories for Places of Interest are: 

* **Areas:** These are smaller zones inside a region. Think of them like a "mini-region” with something intriguing about it, like a single barren patch in a huge forest or a small island in the middle of the sea. Jot or mark that down. It can span or straddle multiple regions if that makes sense.   
* **Settlements:** These are basically anywhere people live, like a little hamlet, a castle, or a big city. Give it a name and pick where it goes. If you’re drawing a blank on a name, see the table below.  
* **Landmarks:** These can be almost anything where something interesting is happening, mundane or magical. It could be anything from an odd formation in the landscape, to a wizard’s tower, to a wandering torrent of darkness that roams the land. Name it and place it somewhere in a region. 

## Step 3: Personal Places

Now, starting with the eldest character, everyone (except the GM) is going to give a specific location \- either a place they call home or a place that holds some significance to them. This place can be somewhere you’ve already established or it can be a brand new Place of Interest. Name it and add it to your world\! Again, if you need help naming, use the tables below. 

For this personal place of significance, name one event that happened there that was important to your character. Something that shaped who they are today. A celebration, a tragedy, or maybe their catalyst for adventure. If you need help on this, refer to the questions you answered during character creation. 

## Step 4: Create Connectors

Now start with a player who hasn’t gone first yet and keep rotating clockwise. This time, you’ll be adding important connectors to your world. A connector is anything that helps people or things from A to B. That could be a road, a river, a secret path, interplanar portals, or an arcane ley line. These connectors can stretch between different regions and can even head off into parts unknown. 

When it’s your turn, decide where the path goes and give it a name. Remember water flows from high ground to something bigger like a lake or ocean (unless it is magical\!) and settlements of people can usually be found near water. 

Add connectors until you have five or so marked. All of these things you’ve added are not a finite list, you can add more of any of them as your adventure plays out. 

## Step 5: Start Rumors

Starting with whatever character has the highest Guile, each player (except the GM) says a rumor their character has heard about any place on the map. It might be true, it might be false, but they’ve heard it said somewhere. The rumor should be interesting enough that heroes or villains might seek it out. The other players at the table can ask any clarifying questions if needed.

## Clear a Condition

Some moves and items in the game will help you clear conditions (such as Make Camp, Take Solace, and ITEM). However, the most direct way to clear a condition is to, in fiction, take a particular action related to that Condition’s emotional state. Each Virtue’s Condition has its own emotional state. At the end of any scene in which you took one of the corresponding actions, declare you’ve done so and clear that condition. 

Below is the list of Virtues, their Conditions, and how to clear them.   
**Might (Exhausted):** Recover by indulging in a vice (like drinking or gambling) and ignoring your responsibilities or duties.  
**Mettle (Afraid):** Flee or avoid a difficult situation rather than confronting it. Whether that's running from a monster or dodging an awkward conversation.  
**Heart (Guilty):** Absolve yourself by facing a danger alone, inflicting a punishment on yourself, or shifting the blame onto a teammate involved in the situation.   
**Wit (Angry):** Vent your rage by hurting someone (physically or emotionally) or destroying something truly important to you or your character.  
**Guile (Insecure):** Overcome your self-doubt by taking a sudden, reckless action or big risk without consulting or informing the rest of your party.

## Marking Beyond Your Final Condition

If you need to mark a Condition but all Conditions are already marked, you Crumble. You lose consciousness, flee, or otherwise *must* leave the scene. Say how you do this and then clear one Condition. Discuss with the GM how and when you’ll return. 

If you are in Combat when you Crumble, you gain *Vulnerable* *4*. You can only take actions that result in you fleeing or staying put. You may gain *Unconscious* or *Incapacitated*. 

# Statuses & Status Limits

Statuses represent a character or NPC’s current banes and boons, ranging in rank from 1 (mild) to 6 (deadly or transformative). Statuses can be used in many ways, such as:

* To track wounds and injuries (bleeding, poisoned)  
* To compel other characters to act (convinced, charmed)  
* To show mood, feelings, and disposition (friendly, raging)  
* To represent tactical advantages and disadvantages (prone, distracted, off-balance)  
* To track progress, for example, of a ritual (progressing)  
* To boost (buff) your allies (blessed, hastened) and hinder (debuff) your foes (hobbled, drained)

On your Playbook, under statuses, you will see something that looks like this:

Example Statuses: 

**Negative Statuses**  
Doomed: You’re in grave danger.   
Impaired: You’re slowed or off-balance.  
Trapped: You’re completely helpless  
Stunned: You’re caught off-guard.

**Positive Statuses**  
Empowered: Your abilities are enhanced in some way.   
Favored: A person or a group demonstrates approval for you.   
Inspired: You’re ready to stand for something.  
Prepared: You’re ready for what’s coming.

Marking & Resisting

* When taking a Status, you may **Resist** by rolling \+ Relevant Virtue \+ Relevant Skill.  
  * On a success, subtract 1 Rank per point of the rolled Virtue.   
  * On a 10+, subtract one additional Rank.   
  * On a miss, take the full effect.  
* You can reduce a status to Rank 0 this way, avoiding it all together.   
* The number you are left with is the Rank you mark on your status tracker. If it is a new status, label the status and add its Rank under the appropriate column (whether it is positive or negative for you). 

Stacking & Reducing

* Gaining the same or similar Status stacks its Rank. Mark the box corresponding to the new Rank, or the next empty box to the right if that Rank is already marked.  
  * The GM will say if a Status stacks.   
  * For instance, an opponent who is Distracted 2 can be made even more distracted by giving them a new distracted status. The GM rules on which statuses stack and which exist independently. For example, wounded and bleeding may stack, because they are similar.   
* The Rank of a Status is always the highest box marked on its Tracker.  
  * For example, if you already were Distracted 2 (and marked the second box) and then gained Distracted 4, add a mark in the fourth box. The new Rank is 4  
  * If a player with Distracted 2 \[Distracted: □X□□□\] gains Distracted 4, it looks like this: \[Distracted: □X□X□\]  
* The way it works means that each player can only take one Rank of each type of Status. Taking Distracted 3 doesn’t make you mark 3 *more* boxes, it makes you check Box 3 OR the first available box to the right of Box 3\.   
* To reduce a Status, clear marks equal to the reduction, starting from the highest box. If reduced below 1, the Status is completely removed.  
  * For example, reducing blessed-4 by 3 ranks results in blessed-1.  
    * \[Blessed: XXXX□\] is reduced by three ranks and becomes \[Blessed: X□□□□\]   
* You can use a **Healing Surge/Recovery** to clear 1D6 \+ Mettle Ranks of any Status.

Using Statuses in Play

* On every roll, add the Rank of your most helpful Status to the situation, and subtract the Rank of your most hindering Status. All other Statuses are ignored even if they are relevant.   
  * If you are bleeding and try to attack or move stealthily, your Bleeding 2 Status would be \-2 from your roll.   
  * If you are protected by a magic barrier and leap through a waterfall of acid, your Protected 3 Status would be \+3 to your roll.   
* The GM can create or remove statuses as consequences by way of a soft move, hard move, or from the consequences of a mixed success or failure. Positive statuses may be created in the case of mixed successes or successes.   
* Some Moves allow you to spend Hold you get from making Moves to create statuses, most often one for one, both positive and negative.   
* Opposite or Inverse Statuses (e.g., Hot/Cold) cancel out: subtract the lower Rank from the higher Rank and keep the prevailing Status.  
  * Hostile 3 \+ Friendly 2 \= Hostile 1 (The Hostile status was higher, so it stays.)  
  * Hostile 3 \+ Friendly 4 \= Friendly 1 (The Friendly status was higher, so the tag flips.)  
* Behavioral Statuses like Charmed, Enraged, or Hypnotized force a character toward a specific behavior or directive.  
  * Any roll made while acting against them subtracts the Rank from your roll if it is the highest related Status.  
  * Once the Status reaches its Limit (box 5), your Hero can no longer resist. They must follow the directive.  
  * Once the Status exceeds the Limit, the status becomes permanent. Drastic measures must be taken to bring them back to their senses.   
* It is up to the player to play out the status in the fiction itself. 

Limits, Scars, & Death

* Statuses (Positive and Negative) cannot normally exceed Rank 5\.   
* Reaching Rank 6 of a Negative Status renders you **Subdued**, preventing actions related to that Status.   
* In the short term, reaching a Negative Status Limit will often dramatically turn a scene, raising the stakes or creating a new challenge to deal with.   
* In the long term, reaching a Negative Status Limit may have long lasting or permanent effects on you. Your Hero may day or be forever changed.   
* If a Subduing consequence is fatal, you must choose to Take a Scar, Risk Death, or go out in a Blaze of Glory.  
* Scars are near-permanent changes; if your total Scars exceed your Playbook level, your character must retire.  
* Risking Death requires a Roll \+ Nothing, though allies can spend Rapport to assist. A 10+ leaves you alive at Rank 3, a 7-9 forces a Scar and unconsciousness, and a 6- means imminent death.

Sometimes, when you are Subdued, there may be long lasting or permanent effects on you. When you are Subdued and the consequences of that Status could lead to death or some permanent Scar, that player and the GM discuss the result. The player will then decide if they **Take A Scar**, **Risk Death**, or go out in a **Blaze of Glory.** 

If you Take a Scar or Risk Death and survive, you either fall unconscious, begin to flee, or otherwise start removing yourself from the conflict at hand. You may return to your senses if your party can remove X Ranks (think about it) of a Negative Status. OR maybe, once you are Subdued, you are removed from the scene and can not return, Combat included. Can a bad guy just cu de gra your downed body??? 

**Scars**  
When your character takes a Scar they undergo a near permanent change \-  spiritually, mentally, or physically. A good scar should alter the way the character interacts with the world, how they are perceived, or shift their personality in some significant way. If your number of Scars is ever higher than your Playbook level, the time has come to end that character’s time with the party. Work with the other players and the GM to find a fitting way for that to happen and create a new character to join the party at whatever level feels appropriate.  

Example Scars & Statuses they came from: 

* Limb loss, body scarring (*Wounded 6\)*  
* Eyes turn pitch black, haunted by constant nightmares (*Cursed 6*)  
* Ostracized by a town or loved one (*Spurned 6\)*  
* Become a Vampire (*Enthralled 6\)* 

Some Scars may be able to be reversed through great feats. The party might choose to retrieve their comrades' souls from the underworld, defeat the wicked magician to dispel the curse, or find a way to redeem their friend in the hearts of the people. Scars provide moments for engaging drama and character driven narratives within the story. 

**Death**  
If you **Risk Death**, roll+Nothing. Other Characters can spend Rapport at twice the cost (2 Rapport for \+1) as it takes considerable effort, attention, and emotional strain in this moment.  

* On a 10+, you live…for now. Set the Status that Subdued you to Rank 3\.   
* On a 7-9, you live, but fall unconscious or are otherwise taken out. The GM decides what Scar you’ll take. Accept this consequence or perish.   
* On a 6-, you’re going to die shortly. What are the last things you experience? What are your last words? 

**Blaze of Glory**  
If you go out in a **Blaze of Glory**, briefly take control of the scene and describe one, last heroic achievement you make, significantly impacting the narrative. Then your character dies. Describe how that happens. 

**Total Party Subdual**   
If the party is in a situation which results in all of them breaking a Limit and becoming Subdued, they are placed at the mercy of their enemies or their environment. The GM takes a hard move that may result in death, capture, or new and dire stakes. 

**Resurrection**   
Should a character die, resurrection is rare, arduous, and often costly. The GM will outline a Clock (or which may contain Clocks itself) of the steps needed to perform a resurrection. Once the clock is complete and the resurrection ritual is performed, the player of the character who is being resurrected has the final say if the resurrection works or if their character remains dead. 

**Enemy Limits**

* Enemy Limits dictate how they are overcome and are written as "\[Rank\] \[Status\]" (e.g., 4 Hurt).  
* Immunities are listed with no maximum Limit (e.g., A ghost might be immune to physical harm. Written out like \~Physical Harm).  
* A single enemy can often be defeated in different ways (e.g., a boar can be Hurt, Scared, or Bonded), but the numerical Limit for each may vary (4 Hurt, 5 Scared, or 3 Bonded).

# Resistance

When you would take a status, you may make a Resist Roll. Roll \+ Relevant Virtue \+ Relevant Skill(s) 

* On a success, \-1 Status Rank per point of Virtue you rolled with (if you rolled with Might, that Virtue is \+2, and you succeeded, \-2 Status Rank).   
* On a 10+ subtract one additional Status Rank.   
* On a miss, you take the full effect.   
* You can reduce a status to Rank 0 this way, avoiding it all together.   
* The number you are left with is the Rank you mark on your status tracker. If it is a new status, label the status and add its Rank under the appropriate column (whether it is positive or negative for you).   
* Some Statuses can’t be resisted. The GM will tell you which. 

# Armor

There are three types of armor Playbook Abilities or Items can give you: Physical, Heavy, and Special. Any time you would take a Status you can mark an appropriate Armor Box to negate that status completely. 

**Physical Armor** can be marked to stop attacks on your body \- things like chainmail or a shield.   
**Heavy Armor** is for those who don heavy armor like Plate Mail. It acts as an additional use of Physical Armor.   
**Special Armor** can be marked to stop attacks of the mind \- it is often represented by magical abilities or items. 

When an armor box is marked, it can’t be used again until it’s restored. All of your armor is restored when you **Make Camp.** Armor usually counts against your total Load. 

In Combat, Armor requires spending an Action Point as a reaction. 

# Load & Item Charges

# Load

On your Journey, you have potential access to all of the items on your character sheet as well as any item that makes sense for your Hero to have brought with them from Camp. 

After you **Make Camp**, decide what your character’s Load will be. While on your Journey you can declare, at any time, that your character has any item they have access to, or any item that would make sense for them to have, on their person right now by checking a Load Box \- up to a number of items equal to your chosen Load. Your Load also determines your speed and conspicuousness. 

* 3 Load is Light. You have \+1 Movement in Combat, gain *Inconspicuous 1\.*   
* 5 Load is Normal. You move normally and look like a prepared adventurer.   
* 6 Load is Heavy. You have \-1 Speed in Combat, and gain *Conspicuous 1\.* 

Your character’s Light, Normal, and Heavy Loads are modified by your Might. Increase your Hero’s Light, Normal, and Heavy Load by your Might score. 

Your Load represents how obvious and bulky your gear is to anyone looking at you. Some items you add to your Camp/Inventory are small or inconsequential and may cost 0-Load and don't count against your limit. Most standard equipment, such as your sword, shield, or armor, are clearly visible and cost 1-Load each. Particularly large or cumbersome items might cost 2-Load. 

Once you check your last Load box, you can not have any new items on you until you **Make Camp**.

## Assess the Situation (Wit)

When you **assess the situation** to figure out what is going on in a moment of risk or conflict, roll \+ Wit. If no risk or conflict are present, say how you do it to ask the GM any question. 

* **On a 10+**, hold 3\. Take \+1 ongoing while acting on any answers.   
* **On a 7-9,** hold 1 or hold 2 and choose one complication. Take \+1 ongoing while acting on any answers.   
* **On a 6-** you find your answer, but it is the worst possible news. Mark XP and the GM takes or holds a hard move.

* **On a 6-**, the answer reveals how bad things really are or a danger you’re barreling toward. The GM makes a move.

## Sway the Spirit (Heart)

When trying to **Sway the Spirit** by using empathy, honesty, or comfort to get someone to do what you want, roll \+Heart. 

* **On a 10+**, they do as you ask to the best of their ability. If you’ve asked too much, they’ll tell you what it would take for them to do it.  
* **On a 7–9**, they are willing, but the GM will choose one:  
  * Gain *Indebted 2\.* You owe them a promise, a payment, or a favor in return. The more you ask, the more they’ll ask in return. They may demand it upfront.   
  * They only follow through on a portion of your request or will only do it after revealing a hard truth to you. GM’s choice.   
  * The act or their answer puts them in danger; take \-1 forward with them due to the friction.  
* **On a 6-**, you reveal a vulnerability or offend them. If they choose to do what you ask, the favor you must do for them is particularly costly, challenging, or undignified. In any situation, the GM makes a Hard Move.

On a Player: 

* **On a 10+** they mark experience or Kin between you and get \+1 forward if they do what you ask.  
* **On a 7-9,** they mark experience or Kin between you if they do what you ask.  
* On a **6-**, it’s up to that player to decide how badly you offend or annoy them. Remove one Kin between you. They mark experience if they **do not** do what you asked. 

## Strike a Nerve (Guile)

When trying to **Strike the Nerve** by using manipulation, provocation, or threats to get someone to do what you want or force a reaction, roll \+Guile.

## Push Yourself

During the game, when one of your skills applies to any roll you are about to make, you can use it to demonstrate your proficiency. Before rolling you can declare your skill to add its \+1 modifier to the roll. If more than one of your skills applies, you may **Push Yourself** to mark one Condition and add an additional \+1 to your roll. You can only Push Yourself once per roll. 

## Recall a Flashback

At any point (maybe outside of Combat) a player can Recall a Flashback to take an action in the past that has relevance to their current situation. To Recall a Flashback, the action you want to take must make sense to have happened. 

Recalling a Flashback gives you the negative status, *Strained.* The Rank you take reflects the severity of the action you “took” in the past. If the action you take in the past would call for a Move, you still roll for that Move and consequences happen accordingly. You can not roll Resistance against this. 

* ***Strained 1\.*** Any ordinary action that you could have easily done.   
* ***Strained 2\.*** An action with the possibility of complication or some unlikely opportunity.   
* ***Strained 3+.*** An intricate or detailed action or set of actions that involved specific opportunities or circumstances. 

You can not Recall a Flashback to “undo” a consequence or something that has already occurred.

Any and all party members can spend Rapport from the Track to help on that same roll. Each teammate can only spend one Rapport themselves. Using Rapport this way can take a total miss to a complete success\!

  * Clear 1D6 Conditions

* Now, each player can take as many Camp Actions as Party Level \+1. Camp Actions:

## Keep Watch

If you Make Camp somewhere potentially dangerous or vulnerable, before heading out on the next leg of your journey, the Party can decide to Keep Watch. If you do, first, the players choose a volunteer to lead the watch and the **GM rolls \+Nothing** and may reveal the result. Once the watch begins, the GM can make their move from the list at any time. 

**On a 10+**, the night passes without incident.  
**On a 7-9**, the GM chooses 1 from the list below.

* The person on watch notices something interesting nearby.   
* One party member of the GM’s choice doesn’t get much sleep. They awake with *Restless 2\.*   
* Something dangerous approaches. If the player on watch has *Alert* or similar, they may rally the party in time. 

**On a 6**, everyone marks Potential, and a danger will emerge tonight.

While you and any other party members Keep Watch, the threats (should they come at all) may not appear immediately. Feel free to describe what you do. Where you go. How you behave. If you are watching with someone else, feel free to chat about anything on your mind or heart. 

On a 10+ Choose 2\. On a 7-9 choose 1\.

* **You choose the turf.** You get the drop on what’s coming and are able to set up where you choose.

## Level Up

**Level Up**  
After you Make Camp, if you have 5 or more Potential, you may reduce your Potential by 5 to increase your Level by 1\. 

The Advancements available to you are listed on your Playbook. You are restricted to the Tier 1 options at first; however, once you have taken four advancements from Tier 1 and reach level 5, you unlock the Tier 2 options. Once you have taken 3 more advancements of either Tier 1 or Tier 2, and reach level 8, you may now also choose from the Tier 3 options. Once you have taken 2 more advancements of Tier 1, 2, or 3, and reach level 10, you may now take Tier 4 options for any remaining Level Ups. 

In fiction, at the Camp, you may choose to describe how the party sees your new advancement. 

## Progress the Party

After you Make Camp, if your Party has 5 or more Rapport, you may choose to reduce your Rapport by 5 to increase your Party Level by 1\. 

The Party Advancements available to you are listed on your Party Playbook. You are restricted to the Tier 1 options at first; however, once you have taken four advancements from Tier 1 and reach party level 5, you unlock the Tier 2 options. Once you have taken 3 more advancements or either Tier 1 or Tier 2, and reach party level 8, you may now also choose from the Tier 3 options. Once you have taken 2 more advancements of Tier 1, 2, or 3, and reach party level 10, you may now take Tier 4 options for any remaining Level Ups. 

In fiction, at the Camp, you may choose to describe how you establish your new advancement. 

## Forge a Bond

After you Make Camp, if you and another PC have 5 or more Kin, you may agree to reduce your Kin by 5 to increase your Bond Level by 1\. Bond Level maxes out at 5\. Each Bond Level has moves and abilities associated with it. Take one available to your new Bond Level or lower. 

In fiction, at the Camp, you may choose to play out a scene demonstrating the connection you two hold to solidify your new Bond Level and/or demonstrate your new advancement. If you do so, mark one potential each.

# Combat Basics

# Combat Loop

1. If the Heroes initiate Combat, add 1 Rapport to the track.  
   1. If all Heroes share the same goal for the fight, add another Rapport.  
   2. If the Heroes did not initiate Combat and are ill-prepared or off-balance, remove 1 Rapport instead.  
2. The GM determines and states the Combat Goal.  
   1. Examples: eliminate the enemies, recover the item, cross the bridge, etc.  
3. When the Heroes achieve the Combat Goal, Combat ends. Each player marks Potential, and the Heroes describe how all remaining enemies are dispatched in the fiction.  
   1. The Heroes and GM may agree to change the **Combat Goal** at any point during Combat.  
   2. If a minority of Heroes pursue a goal different from the majority's, they may state it as a **Defiant Goal**.  
      1. If the Defiant Goal is achieved before the Combat Goal, Combat still ends, and the Defiant Heroes describe what happens in the fiction.  
4. If all creatures on one side are surprised, the other side acts first.   
   1. At the GM's discretion, surprise may give the other side a full round before the surprised side acts, cause the surprised side to start with fewer actions or roll with disadvantage in the first round, or impose a similar effect that fits the fiction.  
5. If neither side is surprised, roll 2D6.  
   1. On 7+, the Heroes act first. On 6-, the enemies act first.  
6. The side acting first chooses one unit to act. Then the other side chooses one unit. Continue alternating sides until every unit has acted.  
   1. If one side has more units, its remaining units act consecutively after the alternating turns end.  
   2. Heroes may choose for two Heroes to move together on a Hero turn. Two enemy units then move together on the enemy's next turn.  
7. This completes the round. Begin the next round with the same side that began Combat.  
   1. Units do not have to act in the same order as they did the previous round.   
   2. Heroes should choose the order each round that best fits their current strategy.

# What Can A Hero Do? 

\*Design Note: Hero Status Ranks currently cap at 6\. Does that still make sense? As a comparison, enemy Statuses range below and above 6 to allow more damage variance in Combat. Maybe Status limits go up as they level up??? 

Moves in Combat do not award Potential on a 6-. Combat rolls cover smaller actions than rolls outside Combat, so they occur more often. When the Heroes achieve the Combat Goal, they all mark Potential.

Each Hero has 3 **Action Points (AP)**. In Combat, Moves taken on their own or another's turn cost 1 AP by default; some cost more. AP recharge at the end of that Hero's turn.

On a Hero's turn, they can:

* Make a Basic Move (1 AP)  
* Make a Combat Move (1 AP each)  
* Make a Hero Move (AP as defined by Move)  
* Make as many Free Actions as the fiction allows (open doors, take an item from a backpack, shout across the battlefield, use a consumable). 

When it is not the Hero’s turn they can: 

* Make a Reaction Move  
* Make a Hero Move marked as a Reaction. 

## Combat Moves

For Engage in Melee & Engage at Range, the Hero can inflict whatever Status is appropriate for the fiction of their character. 

* **Engage in Melee** (Single Target, Range 1\)  
  * Roll 2D6 \+ Might.  
    * 10+: Apply *Status 5*.  
    * 7-9: Apply *Status 4*.  
    * 6-: Apply *Status 3*.  
* **Engage at Range** (Single Target, Range 10\)  
  * Roll 2D6 \+ Might.  
    * 10+: Apply *Status 4*.  
    * 7-9: Apply *Status 3*.  
    * 6-: Apply *Status 2*.  
* **Maneuver**   
  * Move up to 6 spaces in any direction.  
    * Cannot pass through enemies.  
    * Cannot end movement in the same space as an ally or enemy.  
    * Difficult terrain or climbable surfaces count as 2 spaces.   
* **Shift**  
  * Move up to 2 spaces in any direction.   
    * Does not trigger Opportunity Attacks.   
    * Difficult terrain or climbable surfaces count as 2 spaces.   
* **Recuperate**  
  * Spend 1 **Recovery** to clear 1D6 \+ Mettle Ranks from a single Status.

## Reaction Moves

* **Defend**  
  * Mark the appropriate Armor to negate a Status that would be inflicted on you.  
* **Resist**  
  * Reduce the distance of forced movement by up to your Mettle.  
* **Opportunity Attack**  
  * Make the Engage in Melee Move against an adjacent enemy that voluntarily moves out of a space adjacent to you.  
* **Interpose**  
  * When a creature within 2 spaces of you would take a Negative Status, you may push them out of the way and take the Status instead.  
  * Move into their space, then move them into an adjacent space of your choice.  
  * You cannot Resist the incoming Status.  
* **Help**  
  * Spend 1 Rapport from the Rapport Track.   
  * Give another party member \+1 on a Move they have made, even after they roll.  
    * Before granting the \+1, describe how you help.  
  * Multiple Heroes can use the Help Move on the same roll.   
  * A Hero may only use the Help Move once per roll. 

# Gambits

On a 10+ on any Move made in Combat, a Hero may perform any number of **Gambits** that fit the circumstances, marking one Condition per Gambit. On a 12+, they may perform one additional **Gambit** for free. On a 7-9, they may perform only one **Gambit** by marking two Conditions.

* **Bolster.** \+1 Rank to any Status you deal.  
* **Press.** Shift up to 2 spaces even if you are currently unable to move.  
* **Repel.** Force an enemy away from you a number of spaces equal to the Rank of its highest Negative Status.  
* **Halt.** Stop an enemy from moving spaces on its next turn, giving it Rank 2 of an appropriate Status.  
* **Seize.** Take something from an enemy.   
* **Impede.** Describe how you preoccupy or stop an enemy (such as grappling, distracting, or provoking it), then give it Rank 2 of an appropriate Status.  
* **Calculate.** See a weakness in the enemy’s plan. Take \+1 forward or give \+1 forward to an ally.   
* **Brace**. Reduce all Status Ranks inflicted against you by 1 until your next turn.   
* **Other.** Create an effect with a similar level of impact (push, shove, grapple, etc.).

# Cover, Hiding, and Tactics

A unit obscured from its enemies gains the Positive Status *Cover*, with a Rank determined by the circumstances. The same applies to Statuses such as *Invisible* or *Hidden*. Subtract the highest Rank of the most applicable Status from the enemy's roll.

# Unstable

When a Hero reaches *Rank 4* of any Status, they mark Unstable. Being Unstable has no mechanical effect on its own, but interacts with other abilities and moves. 

Enemies become Unstable when one of their Negative Statuses reaches half of its maximum. 

# Recoveries & Recuperation 

Recoveries represent how many times a Hero can recover and press on. Each Hero starts with 6 (or 8?) Recoveries. You can spend Recoveries to remove Negative Statuses. When you have no Recoveries left, take the Exhausted Condition.

When you Recuperate, spend 1 Recovery to clear 1D6 \+ Mettle Ranks from a Status. Some Hero Moves provide other ways to spend Recoveries.

By default, Enemy units use only the actions listed on their stat block.

# The Gameplay Loop

# The Gameplay Loop

**0\. Cold Open/Prologue:** Briefly establish off-screen threats, enemy movements, or the consequences of past actions to set the tone.

**1\. The Setup**

* **Frame the Scene:** Describe the sensory details (who, what, where, when, and why) of where and how we find our Heroes. As a unit or individually.   
  * Good Scene framing often means start late and leave early. Jump straight to the most interesting moment (which could be minutes, days, or weeks after the previous scene/session).   
  * Draw them in using the tension of incoming badness, dynamic  action or Combat, or an important event/bit of dialogue to react to.   
* **Prompt:** You’ve set the situation. Now ask, *“What do you do?”*

**2\. The Action**

* **Establish Intent:** Players describe their actions. If they are vague, ask: *“What are you specifically trying to accomplish?”0*  
* **Provide Context:** Players need complete information to make meaningful choices. If they are stuck, use the environment or NPCs to give them the context they need.  
* **Determine Risk:** Work with the players to figure out what tools their characters have at their disposal. If there is no inherent risk or time pressure, they succeed automatically. If there is a risk, a Move is triggered. Depending on the situation, it may be a Basic Move, Adventure Move, or Hero Move (replacement for Playbook Move).

**3\. The Mechanics**

* **Establish the Stakes:** Before rolling, tell the players the potential cost (e.g., spending a resource, marking a condition, or taking a Status Rank). You can be as general or specific about the cost as you’d like at this stage.   
* **Resolve the Move:** Roll the dice and follow the rules of the triggered Move.

**4\. The Consequences** *Every action, successful or not, should have an immediate or lasting (or both\!) impact that drives the narrative forward. Follow the consequences laid out by the Move.*

* **On a Success (Full or Mixed):** Move the stakes in a positive direction, offer new information, soften a threat, or resolve a problem.  
* **On a Failure (or Mixed Consequence):** They might still complete the action, but they pay the price. Make a **Hard Move**: create a new problem, force a hard choice, escalate an existing threat, or deliver on the established risk.

**5\. Reframe and Repeat**

* **Set the New Stage:** Things look different now. Describe the new reality of the scene based on the consequences of the players' actions.  
* **The Prompt:** Ask again, *“What do you do?”*

Note that during any of these steps, if you feel at a loss for an interesting choice to make, you can always ask your players. “Where would you like to be right now?” “What would make your character hesitate to take this deal?” “Who would your character least like to see in this place?”

# Preparing the First Session

# The First Session

The first session sets the direction for your campaign. Keep track of unresolved threats and dangerous elements to fuel future sessions.

Start *in media res* with a tense or action-packed situation (e.g., an ambush, an infiltration, a summons from the Duke). Immediately ask leading questions like, "Who is ambushing you?" “What did you do to end up here?” to tie the characters directly to the conflict.

As play begins, let players narrate their actions while you tell them which rules or "moves" trigger. If a player just names a move (e.g., "I Invoke Expertise"), ask them to describe *how* they do it.

## First Session Goals:

* **Describe Details:** Ground the world with brief, vivid details that you can expand on later. Nothing is real until you say it outloud, so no need to know *everything* up front.   
* **Use What They Give You:** Skip heavy prep. Build the world (often as you play) using the players' answers, character bonds, and decisions.  
* **Ask Questions:** If you need more information or get stuck, ask specific questions. Ask characters how they react to each other or how they feel in a certain situation or about an NPC or event.   
* **Leave Blanks:** Keep parts of the world undefined so you have room for cool ideas later. Always allow for the players to help fill in the blanks\!   
* **Note Interesting Facts:** Write down any cool lore or ideas the players casually mention. They are seeds for worldbuilding.  
* **Teach the Moves:** Listen for narrative triggers and guide players through the mechanics until they learn them.  
* **Let Everyone Shine:** Present a fantastic world with open-ended challenges so every character's unique skills get a spotlight.  
* **Introduce Lively NPCs:** Make characters dynamic, but don't protect them. A dead NPC can drive the plot just as well as a living one.

Importantly, you should never plan out any actual story in advance. You’ll create Villains, NPCs, and Locations that are connected to the Heroes \- as antagonists, as figures from their backgrounds, as their friends, mentors etc. \- and then use them to present conflict and choice.

The story will come naturally from the Heroes trying to navigate the situations they are in and to figure out who they actually want to be in this world you make together. 

# Adventures

Heroes are at the center of the story, but how do we make them shine? Send them on Adventures\!. An Adventure might last one, ten, or even more sessions. There is no prescription of how long the Adventure will last. So long as the pacing feels right and everyone at the table is having fun, the Adventure is going well. 

Adventures are the framework in which we can allow the Heroes to defend the innocent, pursue Villains and Threats, solve mysteries, take on important quests, and free those in need from the clutches of treachery. It's no fun if they can do those things without conflict, without obstacles. So here is how we create that while also not planning or scripting everything they’ll face before you have time to discover it in play. 

An Adventure is made up of a

* Concept  
* Type  
* Hook  
* Villain  
* NPCs  
* Locations  
* Secrets  
* Countdown

The **Concept** is your “main idea” of your Adventure. Your premise. The conflict (and therefore the fun) of the Adventure is going to be built off the back of your Concept. This will be the core challenge that the Heroes will address. They may learn of and deal with other threats during the Adventure, but the Concept is where everything on an Adventure stems from. 

*Concept \- In a small hamlet, a pack of goblins, led by Grizza the Tall, steal the blacksmith’s daughter in order to sacrifice her in an appeasement to their god in hopes of cleansing the ancient tomb they’ve recently overtaken.* 

The **Type** of Adventure you run determines what kinds of activities the Heroes will engage in. The Adventure’s Type helps you, the GM, figure out what kind of obstacles to put in their way and what kinds of scenes to frame. Choose one of: 

* The Offensive  
  * The Heroes are beset against direct, forceful actions where violence or overwhelming power is the primary solution.   
  * Elements to include: Monstrous creatures or enemies that are armed, dangerous, and intend harm.   
* The Stand  
  * The Heroes must hold the line. A threat is encroaching on a location or corrupting something good, how will the Heroes defend against it?   
  * Elements to include: Enemy invasion, corruption of a leader or group  
* The Race	  
  * The Heroes race against time to catch a quarry, flee from some unstoppable force, or stop something catastrophic from happening (a ritual, spreading fire, etc).   
  * Elements to include: an interesting pursuer or quarry, a very visible countdown to the big bad thing, people or things that make the clock tick faster/slower, obstacles that get in their way.   
* The Mission  
  * The Heroes use stealth, planning, and social maneuvering to bypass what stands in their way.   
  * Elements to include: something to break into or break out of, locations to retrieve or plant important items or rescue someone. Social webs, guards to trick.   
* The Mystery  
  * The Heroes must navigate the unknown by gathering info and intel. Before they’re able to make their next move, they’ll have to figure out exactly what is going on and who the real Villain even is.   
  * Elements to include: Clues and secrets, tight-lipped and loose-lipped NPCs, hidden truths, a big reveal.   
* The Journey  
  * The Heroes must survive in a test against the elements \- either natural or mortal-made. They’ll face down weather, terrain, puzzles, or participate in some structured contest.   
  * Elements to include: Dangerous locations, a game to play, a rival to face, puzzles and traps, magical obstacles guarding the path. 

The **Hook** is your Adventure’s inciting incident. This is an event that disrupts the status quo, starts the main conflict, and sets the Adventure into motion. 

*Hook \- Devastated and desperate for help, Rosa the Blacksmith barges into wherever the Heroes are, pleading for someone capable to travel into the woods and find where the goblins dragged off her daughter.* 

The **Villain**. Behind every Adventure is some sort of Villain. It might be a monster, person, or anomaly. Whatever it is, it is driving the conflict at the heart of the Adventure. A Villain doesn't need to be “killed” or even “defeated” to conclude an Adventure. Their **Goal** must simply be thwarted. In some cases, this will cause a new Goal to be set for the Villain. The start of a new Adventure\! 

A Villain is comprised of: 

* **Name and Aspects.** Choose two or three short, descriptive Aspect tags. Each tag should describe an important or notable physical feature of the Villain. Choose things that will stand out to the Heroes and help you remember how the Villain carries themself.  
* **Goal.** State what concrete objective they are *presently* trying to accomplish.   
* **Scar.** What is it, at their core, that drives their behavior?   
  * A Villain’s Scar is the psychological or environmental wound that has been festering within them for a long time. It colors the way they see the world and act within it. It is the core of their awful behaviors and beliefs.   
    * Ex. To correct my greatest mistake; to prove I deserve the throne; to ensure nobody can abandon me again.  
  * If your Villain is non-sentient or a phenomena, then what are the physical abuses and literal Scars that have caused this effect?   
    * Ex. A forest absorbing harmful fumes from a mad Alcemist’s creations, a village caught in a time loop because of the terrible murder that took place there.   
* **Skill Tags.** 3-5 words or phrases that express the experiences and proficiencies the Villain has gained up to this point. They might be powers, behaviors, or habits. Whatever they are, they help frame your Villain in any scene they are in. Maybe their tags relate to their goal? Maybe they don’t. Examples include:   
  * “Isolated” “well-connected” “always has a plan”   
  * How do these work mechanically?? Same as Heroes?   
* **Resources.** A short list of important NPCs, locations, items, secrets, and ties to the Heroes.    
  * During an Adventure, the PCs may very well learn of these Resources and try to remove them from the Villain’s control.                
* **List Powers.** What unique ways can the Villain impact the world around them even when they aren’t engaged in Combat?   
  * Make them specific and have active effects on the world.   
* **Give them Attacks.** Create or grab two or three Combat Attacks.   
  * Enemy Ability Menu/Builder  
* **Define Resistances and Vulnerabilities.** Record what cannot affect them without special positioning and what can disrupt that protection.  
* **Set Status Limits.** Give the different ways they might be defeated, converted, driven away, contained, exposed, or otherwise removed from the conflict.

* **Goal**: Grizza grew up on the outside of her clan, shunned by the leaders who in turn, turned her friends against her. Now she has convinced this new clan of goblins of her connections to the divine Vuldec, Lord of Goblins and seeks to prove her power and gain a family in return.

**NPCs.** The supporting cast of your Adventure. They may ally with the Heroes or Villain, but either way they help drive the story forward. You don’t need to prepare very many. List out only people who will definitely cross paths with the Heroes on this Adventure. Be they victims or minions of the Villain, witnesses to some important clue, guardians of knowledge or of safety. Think about why this specific person is present in the Adventure and how they’re involved in either the Villain’s plan or the Heroes path. 

An NPC is comprised of: 

* **A name & Aspects.** Choose two or three details that make their presence memorable.  
* **A Type.** An NPC’s Type acts as a quick reference to help you decide their purpose in the story. It isn’t how they act or what they want to be doing but what their function to you as a GM is.  
  * Meddler. To interfere in others’ plans  
  * Minion. To assist in the Villain’s Goal (knowingly or not).   
  * Gossip. To pass on rumors.   
  * Ally. To join the Heroes.   
  * Guard. To protect key places, people, or information.   
  * Opportunist. To swoop in and discredit the Heroes.   
  * Skeptic. To deny the bad things that are happening.   
  * Victim. To always be in danger.   
  * Witness. To reveal information.   
* **A Goal.** A simple statement defining what they want. A Goal helps you know what an NPC will do in any situation.   
  * To be rich; to avenge my father; to expose my rival; spread the word of a god  
* **A Hero Connection.** Not every NPC needs this, but for larger ones, think of an interesting way this NPC can be connected to one or more Heroes’ Background, Goals, or Quests. If connected to more than one Hero, try having those connections stir conflict.   
* **Skill Tags.** 3-5 words or phrases that express the experiences and proficiencies the NPC has gained up to this point. They might be powers, behaviors, or habits. Whatever they are, they help frame your Villain in any scene they are in. Maybe their tags relate to their goal? Maybe they don’t. Examples include:    
  * Speak through dreams.   
  * Silly hat collector.  
  * Summon demons through ritual circles.   
  * Change their face.   
  * Create magic items.   
  * Always hiding from their “enemies.”   
  * Wheeling and dealing.   
  * Pyromaniac.  
  * Holy avenger.   
  * Speaks in code.   
* **Combatant or not?** If your NPC is capable in combat, define their Status Limits (6 for a standard Combatant). If they are not capable, their Status Limits are likely 1 or 2\. 

**Locations**

You can’t prepare every location the Heroes will come across, but having a few core ones ready will help you provide some inspiring places for your Heroes to potentially explore. Define a Location for 3-5 places you expect the Heroes to spend any significant time during the Adventure. A place that is certain to see action, intrigue, or where the Villain or important NPC resides are good places to start. Locations are where your scenes take place and should be places that drive conflict and interesting choices. 

Don’t go overboard, you can always make more between other sessions as you see where the Heroes are going. And if a Location goes unused during an Adventure, save it for the next one\! 

Good ideas of locations to include would be the general town or area, where the Villain resides, places where victims or witnesses of the Villain’s goal reside, places that trusted knowledge or allies can be found. 

A Location is comprised of: 

* **A name & Aspects.** Choose 3 Aspects, each describing an important, notable, or useful feature of the location. These tags should be things that your Heroes can interact with; features that will matter to them.   
* **Location Type.** Choose one.   
  * Nexus: to bring people, magic, and things together.  
  * Deathtrap: to harm intruders  
  * Lair: to contain monsters of evildoers   
  * Citadel: to bar entry  
  * Lab: to create arcane or evil things  
  * Archive: to reveal information   
  * Labyrinth: to befuddle and separate  
  * Gaol: to constrain and bar exit   
  * Wilds: to contain secrets and hidden things. 

Optionally, one or more custom moves.

**Secrets**

Secrets help move an Adventure forward during play. They’re a simple way to create conflict, meaning, and story for the Heroes. They connect NPCs, Locations, Villains, and, well, everything else. 

A Secret is a single, evocative sentence revealing important lore, story leads, or hidden NPC motives. It should be information the Heroes can discover and act on as they explore the world. The information should always matter to them. That might mean information on an NPC that helps them frame that NPCs Goal or a clue that will bring them closer to finding the Villain’s lair. It might also be a detail that will lead them to a powerful magic item. 

During prep, write your Secrets but never plan the explicit way that the Heroes will uncover it. Do not tie Secrets directly to specific NPCs, locations, or items. That way, you don’t feel stuck to only revealing certain pieces of info during certain moments of the game (moments that might never come up\!. 

Because your Secrets are "floating," you can seamlessly drop them wherever the players decide to go. A Secret can just as easily be revealed as idle gossip between NPCs, a journal entry found inside an armoire, or extracted via interrogation from a goblin. This flexibility allows you to adapt to any player choice on the fly while both playing to find out AND appearing like you planned it all along. 

To prep your Secrets: 

Write down 10 Secrets before your Adventure Begins. Any fewer and you might find yourself short handed when you see where your Heroes actually go during the first session. Any more and its just too much to track. If you find that those last few are hard to think of, dig deep\! They might be weird, but those weird ones will often be the most fun. Before each Session, if a secret was revealed, replace it with a new one to get back to 10\. 

Now, you won't reveal them all in a single session, or maybe even over the entire Adventure. And that's fine\! Because Secrets are a sentence or so long, unused ones are not wasted prep. At least you got your brain going. Save those unrevealed ones for the next session or, if they just don’t make sense based on what happened last time, let it fade into the abyss. 

Remember, a secret isn't “real” until the players learn it. If you wrote down that the Villain is actually the scavenger NPC’s grandparent, but the players never find an organic place to discover that, maybe the NPC was a nobody after all… 

A really fun progression with Secrets is that they often naturally evolve into Quests. If the players learn the secret “A mad Warlock is trying to bring forth a devil” the Heroes will likely turn that into the Quest “Stop the Warlock’s ritual.” 

**Adventure Countdown**

​​Each Adventure has a Countdown. That is, the series of events that will play out of the Heroes can’t stop the Villain from achieving their Goal. This countdown will be your go-to guide as to what is happening with the Villain when they are off-screen as the Heroes try and save the day. 

A good countdown will reflect how the world would change if the Heroes were never able to help. What would be destroyed? Who would die? Be captured? How would the Villain go about achieving their Goal? 

A good way to think about your Countdown is to get into the mind of your Villain. Think about what they want, think about the NPCs and Locations you’ve built around them. Now, how would they go about achieving their goals? Each step of the countdown gets closer and closer to the Villain’s ultimate success. Divide those thoughts into the following six steps: 

**Seed.**  
**Bloom.**  
**Wilt.**  
**Wither.**  
**Rot.**

Once you have these noted, you can always refer to the Countdown during the game. When do these events actually happen? Great question. As the Heroes Quest and Adventure through the world, the Villain should always be moving closer to their Goal. Great times to advance your Countdown are: 

* If the Heroes haven’t pursued any hook leading them to foil the Villain’s Goal.   
* When the Heroes become distracted from the Villain’s Goal for too long.   
* Shortly after you use a Cold Open, an NPC, or other way to reveal off-screen badness that warns the players of the next step.   
* A Hero misses a move that has dire consequences and is related to the Adventure Countdown  
* An on screen NPC or Villain succeeds at making a move that would advance the Countdown.   
* When you feel it is appropriate or dramatic\! 

Because this countdown is a list of steps of what *would* happen if the Heroes don’t interfere, you’ll find that you will need to revise the Countdown between sessions based on what occurred during play. The Heroes very likely did some things that prevented parts or all of the way your Villain was pursuing their Goal and now they have to adapt to those pesky adventurers. Some events might stay the same, some might be moved around, or changed entirely \- and that is great\! When prepping your Countdown between sessions, get into the mind of your Villain. How have the Heroes’ actions altered their path to their Goal? And how will they adapt? 

The Countdown will always be your guide of what the Villain is trying to do and can be a great reference to fall back on during play. If you know what the bad guys want, you can always make moves to get them closer to that objective. 

# Clocks

**Clocks** are player-facing circles (or tracks\!) divided into even segments. They help the players and GM abstract out a series of events, a tense scene, or a hefty task. Not all circumstances require a Clock, but using them can help keep complicated within clear parameters, allowing the story to progress at a pace that keeps the pace from moving too fast for things that have weight or too long so that things don’t lose that weight and drag on.

The GM will make a Clock when they need to track an ongoing effort against an obstacle. Note that you only need to make a Clock when failure has consequences. Obstacles that require the success of a single action or move do not need Clocks. Just judge the outcome based on one move or action. Also, the Hero’s actions do not risk the Heroes’ precious time, resources, or some other badness, don’t make a Clock. 

In most cases, when a Clock comes into play, there will be two tracks under the title of the Clock: one labeled Success and one labeled Failure. Both tracks usually start at 0 **Headway**.

All Heroes (and potentially their allies) that are involved in a Clock (no matter if they join it from the start or in the middle) are subject to conclusive consequences of a Clock, for the good or bad. 

*Do* make a Clock when the Heroes are sneaking into a heavily guarded castle. You might create a new Clock called Castle. That Clock gets two tracks \- Success and Failure. Each successful action or move the Heroes make will get them closer and closer to gaining entry. While each failure will get them closer and closer to disaster, however the GM defines. 

Note, that Clocks are defined by their obstacle NOT the method of completion. In this example, the GM could create a Clock for “Guards” and “Tower Gate” not “stealth past the guards” or “break through the tower gate.” The Clock defines what stands in the Heroes’ way, not how the Heroes need to address the obstacles themselves \- that’s up to them\!

More complex problems usually have more segments in their clock. Basic obstacles have 4 segments (requiring at least 2 moves, minimum), with increasing complexity going up in even values.

When a Clock is in play, any Hero can take any action or Move to attempt to progress the Success track of the Clock. Before they do, they say how much Headway they are willing to risk between 1 and 3 and then take an action appropriate in the fiction to that risk (1 being the lowest risk, 3 being the highest risk actions). An action without an associated roll is likely to cap at 1 Headway (GMs discretion). When a roll is made, resolve the effects of that roll. In addition: 

**On a 10+** the Hero’s gain the Headway risked. Increase the Success Clock by that much. 

**On a 7-9** the Hero’s and their antagonist gains the Headway risked. Increase both the Success and Failure Clock by that much. 

**On a 6-** the enemy side gains that Headway. Increase the Failure Clock by that much. 

When any Success Track on a Clock is filled completely, the task or challenge it represents is finished, defeated, or surpassed. The Heroes succeeded\! 

When any Failure Track on a Clock is filled in completely, the opposite is true. The Heroes have failed and the GM can make a Hard Move. 

Losing sides can spend the Headway they *did* make 1-for-1 (maximum 4\) to:

* Learn something important about another side.   
* Find something advantageous after their Failure.   
* Grant Advantage Forward to all Hero’s involved in the Failure.   
* Inflict Disadvantage Forward on all Hero’s on an opposing side.

For complex obstacles, Clocks can be broken out into layers. Each layer would have its own Clock. For example, Count Jemvira’s Villa might have a “Perimeter Guard” clock, a Servants and Staff Clock, and an “Arcane Alarm System” clock. The Heroes would have to progress through all three layers to reach Count Jemvira’s office and the damning evidence of corruption within. However, if and when they do succeed at all three layers (barring some wild circumstance) they should succeed at their task with no further hindrance.

The type of Clock outlined above is called a Basic Clock. It can be used for many challenges such as: chases to or away from something or someone, convincing someone notable of something important, infiltration to something protected, violent skirmishes that don’t require Combat, explicit environmental hazards on a time crunch (Maybe the Hero’s are trying to complete the Success Track on the Hidden Vault Clock open to find the vault on the sinking pirate ship before the Failure Track and the vessel goes down). 

A Story of Heroes and Villains has a few other types of Clocks that you, as the GM, can use for various situations.

**Threat Clocks/Quest Clocks (are these the same thing?)**  
Threats are added during the Adventure when Heroes make enemies, encounter bad stuff, or learn of unresolved story threads they want to pursue. Threats always endanger something the Heroes care about, drive them to make interesting choices, and challenge them during play. Threats could be something that is happening right now or something threatening to come to pass. Either way, the Heroes are going to want to address them.

### **Long-Term Project**

Some projects will take a long time. A basic long-term project (like tinkering up a new feature for a device) is eight segments. Truly long-term projects (like creating a new designer drug) can be two, three, or even four clocks, representing all the phases of development, testing, and final completion. Add or subtract clocks depending on the details of the situation and complexity of the project.

A long-term project is a good catch-all for dealing with any unusual player goal, including things that circumvent or change elements of the mechanics or the setting. For example, by default in the game, trauma is permanent. But maybe a player wants to work on a project where they create a device to draw traumatic spirit-energies into the ghost field, thus reducing a character’s trauma and unleashing a storm of enraged ghosts in the area. It will be a long and dangerous process to set up everything needed to begin and work on a project like this, but almost anything can be attempted as long as the group is interested and it seems feasible to everyone.

### **PROGRESS CLOCKS**

You can also use clocks to track the status or time pressure of a larger ongoing situation in the world or campaign, such as a war, a spreading Blight, a faction’s political influence, or an assassination plot. These progress clocks tend to move by themselves. Tick segments on these clocks when the story or campaign moves forward. A good time to do this is after an interlude. When ticking these clocks, tick one segment if the situation progressed a little, two if it progressed as normal, and three if there was great progress. For example, if you have a clock tracking the progress of a war and it’s drawing to a close, you might tick three segments on that clock at the end of downtime.

### **Linked Clocks**

You can make a clock that unlocks another clock once it’s filled. For example, the GM might make a linked clock called “Trapped” after an “Alert” clock fills up. When you fight a veteran warrior, she might have a clock for her “Defense” and then a linked clock for “Vulnerable.” Once you overcome the “Defense” clock, then you can attempt to overcome the “Vulnerable” clock and defeat her. You might affect the “Defense” clock with violence in a knife-fight, or you lower her defense with deception if you have the opportunity. As always, the method of action is up to the players and the details of the fiction at hand.

### **Mission Clocks**

The GM can make a clock for a time-sensitive mission, to represent the window of opportunity you have to complete it. If the countdown runs out, the mission is scrubbed or changes—the target escapes, the household wakes up for the day, etc.

### **Tug-of-War Clocks**

You can make a clock that can be filled and emptied by events, to represent a back-and-forth situation. You might make a “Revolution\!” clock that indicates when the refugee Skovlanders start to riot over poor treatment in Doskvol. Some events will tick the clock up and some will tick it down. Once it fills, the revolution begins. A tug-of-war clock is also perfect for an ongoing turf war between two crews or factions.

# Future Session Preparation

Now that you’re in the Adventure and have already had a session, you don’t actually need to do too much\! So much of this game is discovering what happens while you are at the table. To prepare for your next session, think about what happened last session. Based on those events:

* **Consider your Heroes.** Look at the Party Goal, current Wants, Quests, significant Bonds, Conditions, and unresolved consequences. What are some ways you can introduce conflict or challenges to where they stand?   
* **Progress the World.** Determine what changed because of the previous session. Which NPCs move closer to or farther away from their Goals? How do they do that? Do they want something different now? How will they react to the actions the Heroes last took. How might they respond?    
  * **Update Your Secrets, Countdown, and Threat/Quest Clocks.** Take a look at what happened in the last session and bring your Secrets list back up to 10 and update your Adventure Countdown and any Clocks needed based on that information. The world adjusts to your Heroes actions\!   
* **Craft a Cold Open and a Strong Start.** Your Cold Open is your chance to reveal any information you want to bring to the front of mind to the players (not the Heroes). Maybe that’s about a Location, an NPC, the Villain, an artifact. Anything\! After your cold Open, you have the chance to set the first scene. You’ll often want to open by presenting them a choice, disturbance, discovery, demand, or visible consequence.  
* **Jot down some possibilities.** Using only a few words, note two or three scenes you can imagine framing, don’t think about what your Heroes might do in them, just dream up scenes that would make sense for them to encounter based on the state of the world.   
* **Get excited.** When you have enough material to respond confidently, leave room for discovery and play\!
