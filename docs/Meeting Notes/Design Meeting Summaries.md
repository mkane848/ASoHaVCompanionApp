### **Rapport overflow**

* Rapport can exceed its normal maximum.  
* Overflow Rapport can be preserved until the party returns to camp, where it may support multiple advancements.  
* Spending Rapport before returning to camp forfeits the overflow and resolves the expenditure from the normal cap.

* This creates a deliberate choice between preserving advancement potential and spending Rapport to survive an immediate problem.

### **Load and inventory**

The group established a strong initial direction for Load:

* Characters select a Load allowance rather than itemizing every ordinary piece of equipment before an adventure.  
* Unused Load slots act as wildcards.  
* During play, a character can declare that they packed a reasonable ordinary item and assign it to an available wildcard slot.  
* This lets characters appear prepared without requiring extensive shopping or packing scenes.  
* Ordinary wildcard items return to the “ether” when Load resets at camp; they do not need to remain permanently recorded.  
* Named, special, magical, or plot-relevant items persist in the inventory and consume Load.  
* A newly acquired named item may therefore force a character to sacrifice some remaining wildcard capacity.  
* Charges can distinguish items that have limited or unusually powerful functions.  
* Ordinary equipment should usually work without detailed resource tracking. Ammunition and similar supplies only become mechanically important when the fiction calls for it.  
* Running out of wildcard Load should create problems and invite other solutions, such as relying on another character, returning to camp, accepting exhaustion, or calling in a favor.

# **Meeting summary — August 25, 2026**

This meeting established a much clearer identity for *A Story of Heroes and Villains*. The team completed a strong first draft of the GM’s adventure-preparation loop, developed the dual-track clock system, and articulated what distinguishes the game from both traditional fantasy RPGs and narrative-first games.

## **The heart of the game**

The clearest statement to emerge was:

> This is the story of disparate people who are not yet heroes becoming heroes—and becoming a found family. Every system should serve that journey.

The game is not primarily about classes, builds, or tactical optimization. Its mechanical center is the story the characters create through:

* Themes  
* Quests  
* Kin and Bonds  
* Rapport  
* Difficult choices  
* The consequences of success, failure, injury, and loss

The story a character builds is itself the advancement system. Abilities and skills should emerge from what that person experiences and becomes, rather than from following a predetermined class progression.

### **What makes it different**

The game is intended to occupy a space between two familiar styles:

* For traditional fantasy players who want more character-driven, narrative play  
* For narrative-game players who want more consequential, decision-rich combat  
* For both groups, a game that avoids excessive crunch without making danger feel toothless

The working shorthand is:

> **A narrative fantasy game with high-stakes, goal-driven combat.**

Combat is not a separate minigame or a mandatory exercise in reducing enemy health. It is a storytelling structure used when the heroes face a meaningful objective under pressure. The important question is not simply “Can we defeat these enemies?” but:

* What are we trying to accomplish?  
* What will happen if we fail?  
* What are we willing to risk?  
* Who do we choose to save?  
* What does that choice reveal about the heroes?  
* How do the characters respond when someone they love is hurt or lost?

A Superman-style example captured the intended experience: the hero can pursue the villain and stop the bomb, or turn aside to save someone in immediate danger. Saving that person may provide a benefit, but the villain’s clock continues. The mechanical decision expresses who that version of Superman is.

### **Found family as a mechanical experience**

The found-family concept is not meant to be flavor layered over a generic fantasy engine. It should determine how the game works.

Individual growth, relationship growth, and party growth should all create both strength and dramatic friction. When someone is injured or dies, the question should not merely be whether the party has access to resurrection. It should be whether they can afford—in practical and emotional terms—to stop everything and bring that person back.

Kin, Bonds, and Rapport should give the party meaningful ways to respond to danger because they care about one another. Those relationships should be tested by competing quests, sacrifices, and situations where saving everyone is impossible.

### **Characters serve their stories**

Characters are not expected to remain in play indefinitely merely because the player has invested advancement in them.

When a character’s arc reaches a satisfying conclusion, the player may be able to:

* Retire them into the world  
* Establish their new role as an NPC  
* Turn them into a party resource or connection  
* Begin a new character and story  
* Potentially bring the retired character back when the fiction calls for it

This reinforces the principle that the character serves the story—not the other way around.

### **A design test for every mechanic**

A proposed system should be able to answer at least one of these questions:

* Does it reveal who this character is becoming?  
* Does it create an interesting dramatic choice?  
* Does it deepen or test the party’s relationships?  
* Does it advance the heroes’ or villain’s objectives?  
* Does it make danger consequential?  
* Does it help the table create the story together?

If it does none of these, it should be reduced, deferred, or removed.

## **Key design decisions**

### **Adventure structure**

An **Adventure** is the main unit of active play: the difficult undertaking between periods of rest, celebration, reflection, or life back in town.

The working adventure framework contains:

1. Concept  
2. Type  
3. Hook  
4. Villain  
5. NPCs  
6. Locations  
7. Secrets  
8. Countdown

The GM prepares conflicts, pressures, and people—not prescribed solutions. Adventure types tell the GM what kinds of obstacles to present, but never dictate how the heroes must overcome them.

### **Adventure types**

The first six types are:

* **Offensive:** Confront direct, forceful, or violent opposition.  
* **Stand:** Hold a position or protect something against encroaching danger.  
* **Race:** Pursue, escape, or act against a visible deadline.  
* **Mission:** Use planning, infiltration, stealth, or social maneuvering.  
* **Mystery:** Investigate clues, hidden truths, and competing accounts.  
* **Journey:** Survive or overcome terrain, weather, hazards, puzzles, or other structured travel challenges.

Each type should include suggested obstacles, scenes, NPC roles, and possibly favored approaches. These are GM prompts rather than mechanical restrictions.

### **Starting-play guidance**

The rules should explicitly answer the frequently neglected question: “What do I do before our first game?”

The eventual GM section should distinguish between:

* Preparing the first adventure  
* Preparing subsequent sessions  
* Preparing the period between adventures

A starter adventure should teach the game through play. Premade adventures and annotated examples can show what the intended experience looks like without requiring GMs to reproduce them exactly.

### **Villains**

A villain is the primary force driving an adventure’s conflict. It can be a person, monster, organization, environmental phenomenon, supernatural anomaly, or anything else capable of pursuing or embodying a harmful objective.

A villain currently includes:

* **Name**  
* **Aspects:** Two or three memorable physical or presentational details  
* **Goal:** A concrete, presently achievable objective  
* **Scar:** The old psychological, social, or environmental wound driving its behavior  
* **Skill tags:** Three to five relevant capabilities, experiences, habits, or powers  
* **Resources:** Important people, places, items, and connections under its control  
* **Powers:** Ways it affects the world outside combat  
* **Attacks:** Two or three options selected or adapted from a menu  
* **Resistances and vulnerabilities**  
* **Status limits and removal conditions**

The villain’s Goal should be specific—steal an artifact, complete a ritual, capture someone—not an indefinite ambition such as “become king.”

A non-sentient villain still has a Scar, expressed as the abuse, corruption, or event that created its present condition.

Villain and player skill tags should operate similarly. When a villain capitalizes on an applicable tag, it should receive the same kind of benefit a hero would. This keeps both sides of the table engaged with a common mechanical language.

### **Concluding an adventure**

An adventure concludes when the villain’s current Goal is decisively thwarted. The villain does not need to be killed, captured, or permanently defeated.

A thwarted villain might:

* Die or be permanently removed  
* Retreat  
* Abandon the current Goal  
* Establish a new Goal  
* Return as the focus of a later adventure

The exact procedure for formally closing an adventure still needs to be written.

### **NPCs**

Only prepare NPCs certain to matter. The recommendation is closer to four purposeful NPCs than a fully populated town.

Each NPC has:

* Name and aspects  
* Story-function type  
* Personal goal  
* Optional connection to one or more heroes  
* Skill tags  
* Combatant status and appropriate status limits

Proposed NPC types include:

* Meddler  
* Minion  
* Gossip  
* Ally  
* Guard  
* Opportunist  
* Skeptic  
* Victim  
* Witness

These types describe the NPC’s function for the GM, not their self-conception or personality.

### **Locations**

Prepare approximately three to five locations likely to host meaningful action, discovery, or conflict.

Each location receives:

* A name  
* Three notable, useful, or interactive aspects  
* A story-function type  
* An optional custom move

Proposed location types include:

* Nexus  
* Death Trap  
* Lair  
* Citadel  
* Lab  
* Archive  
* Labyrinth  
* Jail  
* Wilds

Unused locations should be saved and repurposed rather than discarded.

### **Secrets**

Before an adventure, the GM writes approximately ten short, evocative secrets.

Secrets should:

* Reveal actionable lore, motives, leads, or opportunities  
* Matter to the heroes  
* Connect other pieces of the adventure  
* Remain unattached to a specific discovery method

A secret might be revealed through gossip, a journal, interrogation, environmental evidence, or any other appropriate moment. The GM should not predetermine where the players will find it.

A secret is not canon until the players discover it. Unrevealed secrets can be reused, altered, or abandoned. Revealed secrets may naturally become player quests.

Replace discovered secrets between sessions to return the list to approximately ten.

### **Adventure countdowns**

Every adventure has a Countdown describing what happens if the heroes never stop the villain.

The Countdown:

* Tracks the villain’s off-screen progress  
* Shows how the world deteriorates  
* Helps the GM decide what the villain does next  
* Changes between sessions in response to the heroes’ interference

Good reasons to advance it include:

* The heroes ignore the adventure’s hook  
* They become distracted for too long  
* A miss produces a relevant dire consequence  
* An NPC or villain successfully advances the plan  
* An off-screen scene reveals developing danger  
* Advancement would be appropriate and dramatic

The transcript refers to six stages but names only **Seed, Bloom, Wilt, Wither, and Rot**. The missing stage or intended stage count needs clarification.

### **Basic clocks**

Clocks are player-facing tools for substantial obstacles where failure has real consequences.

Most clocks have two tracks:

* **Success**  
* **Failure**

Characters generate **Headway** by acting against the obstacle. Before rolling, the player chooses to risk one to three Headway:

* **10+:** Add the risked Headway to Success.  
* **7–9:** Add it to both Success and Failure.  
* **6−:** Add it only to Failure.

This makes the degree of commitment a player choice rather than a fixed result.

Additional principles:

* Define a clock by the obstacle, not a required solution.  
* Use “Tower Gate,” not “Break Down the Tower Gate.”  
* Basic obstacles use four segments, with harder obstacles increasing in even increments.  
* Complex situations can use layered clocks.  
* Small skirmishes, chases, persuasion, infiltration, and environmental crises can all use basic clocks.  
* Once a clock’s Success track fills, the heroes have genuinely overcome that obstacle.  
* Filling Failure means the obstacle defeats them and the GM can make a hard move.  
* The losing side may spend earned Headway—up to four—for limited benefits such as learning something, finding an advantage, or influencing what follows.

Status tracks are also clocks, making this system part of the game’s mechanical foundation.

### **Quests**

The group moved away from giving every quest its own clock. That risks burdening the game with too many parallel tracks and does not fit open-ended personal quests particularly well.

The simpler working direction is:

* Quests provide situational benefits, drawbacks, or permissions.  
* Pursuing or resisting a quest affects how a character approaches relevant actions.  
* Some exceptional quests may use clocks, but clocks are not the default.  
* Quests can update as circumstances change rather than simply completing or failing.  
* The interaction between personal quests, party quests, Kin, and Rapport still needs to be designed.

### **Combat**

The game distinguishes between two scales of violence:

* **Lowercase combat:** A short fight or skirmish resolved as an ordinary clock.  
* **Capital-C Combat:** A zoomed-in confrontation with a meaningful objective, real strategic choices, and serious consequences.

Capital-C Combat should always have a goal beyond “eliminate every enemy.” Examples include:

* Rescue someone  
* Stop a ritual  
* Hold a position  
* Steal an object  
* Escape before disaster  
* Expose or contain a villain

When the goal is achieved, the consequential part of combat is over. The table can narrate what happens to the remaining opposition instead of spending another hour resolving a foregone conclusion.

The map or grid exists to establish shared spatial understanding, not to turn movement optimization into the game’s primary challenge.

### **Division of responsibility**

The GM’s job is to present:

* Problems  
* Villain Goals  
* Countdown pressure  
* Important people and places  
* Consequences

The GM should not prepare correct solutions or tailor every obstacle to the heroes’ abilities.

Players are responsible for:

* Recognizing when their abilities and tags apply  
* Tracking Kin, Rapport, and relationship benefits  
* Choosing how to address obstacles  
* Managing the situational advantages created by their stories  
* Deciding how much they are willing to risk

This reduces GM fatigue and gives players responsibility for their own narrative mechanics.

# **Meeting summary — August 27, 2026**

This meeting turned the game’s newly articulated identity into a substantially redesigned character system. The largest breakthrough was replacing playbooks and conventional leveling with **Motifs**: evolving pieces of a hero’s identity, each carrying its own skill, flaw, quest, and advancement.

## **The heart of the game**

The revised design reinforces the central promise established during the previous meeting:

> *A Story of Heroes and Villains* is about disparate people becoming heroes and becoming a found family, expressed through fast, high-stakes, goal-oriented fantasy play.

The basic moves, advancement rules, and character sheet should all focus on that experience. The game does not need a dedicated move for every possible action. Sneaking, for example, can happen through the fiction or an existing move such as **Follow a Lead**. A dedicated move is only necessary when an activity represents something the game is fundamentally about.

What most clearly distinguishes the emerging design is this:

### **Heroes grow by confronting who they are**

Characters do not advance simply because they succeeded, accumulated generic experience, or defeated enough enemies. They grow when they:

* Act through their established strengths  
* Lean into their flaws  
* Fail while attempting something consequential  
* Pursue or deliberately abandon their quests  
* Allow their identities and relationships to change through play

A flaw is therefore not merely a penalty. Invoking it gives the character a disadvantage, but it also produces Potential for that part of their identity. The game rewards players for making dramatically truthful decisions—even when those decisions make success less likely.

### **Character development becomes literal**

A hero’s identity is not a static backstory or class label. When a quest reaches its conclusion, the player rewrites the associated Motif, skills, flaws, and next quest to reflect who the character has become.

The character sheet itself becomes a record of the story:

* “Inheritor” might become “Friend of the Avatar.”  
* A disgraced commander might reclaim leadership—or reject that identity entirely.  
* A skill that once represented inherited authority might become hard-earned strategic insight.  
* A flaw that no longer describes the hero can be removed or rewritten.

The game does not merely say that characters change. It asks players to change the words and mechanics defining them.

### **Growth occurs at three connected scales**

The design now has a clear nested structure:

1. **Hero advancement:** Who am I becoming?  
2. **Party advancement:** What kind of group are we becoming?  
3. **Bond advancement:** What kind of relationship are we building?

These systems should share a recognizable language while becoming progressively lighter. This makes the found-family premise structural rather than decorative.

### **Mechanics should follow the scene**

A roll should never feel like it interrupts roleplay. It should arise naturally when the table reaches uncertainty and wonders what happens next.

Likewise, advancements are primarily reflective. The dramatic scene happens first; afterward, the players recognize that they invoked a flaw, reached a quest milestone, or changed a relationship and mark the appropriate progress.

The working design tests are:

* Keep it simple.  
* Reinforce familiar patterns across systems.  
* Reward players for leaning both into and against their established identities.  
* Never interrupt a scene merely to service a mechanic.

The “Is this game for you?” material and the game’s defining characteristics were also tightened to better communicate its intended audience and experience.

* Statuses  
* Resistances and armor  
* Load  
* Basic moves  
* Adventure moves  
* The existing combat loop

**Aid** was added to the adventure moves as the primary way to spend Rapport to assist another hero.

A future rules review should use missing functionality as a litmus test: if heroes need to do something central to the game but no existing move supports it, the move set may need revision.

### **Playbooks have been replaced**

Hero creation has been rebuilt without classes or playbooks.

The current creation sequence is:

1. Choose name, pronouns, and physical description.  
2. Choose three Motifs.  
3. Give each Motif a skill tag.  
4. Give each Motif a flaw tag.  
5. Write one quest for each Motif.  
6. Assign Virtues and review conditions.  
7. Complete the remaining relationship and party material once those systems are finalized.

Players may select from suggested Motifs and tags or write their own.

### **Motifs**

A hero chooses three **Motifs**, each representing a significant component of their background, identity, experience, or role in the story.

The provided Motifs are intentionally evocative but broad. They communicate a fantasy archetype without prescribing a class, ability package, or personality.

A character might combine concepts such as:

* Inheritor  
* Sworn  
* Mystic  
* Artisan  
* Devoted  
* Exalted

The system supports unusual combinations and fully custom Motifs.

“Motif” remains a working term. **Aspect** is the main alternative under consideration.

### **Skill tags**

Each Motif begins with a skill tag describing a relevant strength, experience, or capability.

Examples for an exiled fire-wielding prince might include:

* Royal Family  
* Tracker  
* Fire Sorcerer

A relevant skill currently provides a positive modifier to a roll. The exact stacking and numerical rules still require testing.

### **Flaw tags**

Each Motif also receives a flaw tag expressing a meaningful weakness, fear, burden, or complication.

When a flaw applies:

* The hero takes a negative modifier.  
* The associated Motif marks Potential whether the action succeeds or fails.

This turns flaws into invitations for dramatic play. Players can deliberately enter situations where their shortcomings matter because doing so produces character growth.

Flaw lists should remain open-ended rather than exhaustive.

### **Potential and Motif advancement**

Potential is tracked separately for each Motif.

A Motif can gain Potential through:

* Missing a noncombat roll  
* Relevant moves  
* Completing or forsaking a quest  
* Acting through an applicable flaw, regardless of success or failure

When a Motif marks its fifth Potential, clear the track and choose an advancement:

* Add a skill tag.  
* Add a flaw tag.  
* Remove a flaw tag.  
* Gain a legal Hero Improvement.  
* Rewrite or update the Motif’s existing skills and flaws to reflect the hero’s current identity.

Hero Improvements will use open, connected advancement trees. Players may develop broadly across multiple trees or progress more deeply through one.

### **Quests are part of the hero**

Quests are no longer external objectives assigned primarily by the GM. Each hero writes one quest for each Motif.

A quest explains what drives that portion of the hero’s identity into adventure, danger, conflict, and change.

Starting quests can follow four forms:

* **Dream:** Something the hero wants.  
* **Tenet:** Something the hero believes.  
* **Safeguard:** Something the hero wants to protect.  
* **Fascination:** A question the hero needs answered.

Quests should be short, actionable statements such as:

* Prove my worth to my family.  
* Find the sacred sword.  
* Free my sister from her captors.  
* Bring the ring to the Forge of Doom.

### **Act Breaks and Forsakes**

Every quest has two potential progressions:

* Three escalating **Act Breaks**  
* Three escalating **Forsakes**

Mark an Act Break when the hero achieves a major success in pursuing the quest. Each successive Act Break must represent a more significant development.

Mark a Forsake when the hero deliberately turns away from the quest. Forsakes also escalate in significance.

If a major fictional event immediately resolves the quest, the rules should not force the player to manufacture additional intermediate steps.

“Act Break” may be shortened to **Act**.

### **Completing a quest**

After the third Act Break:

* Mark enough Potential to trigger advancement for that Motif.  
* Retitle the Motif to reflect the hero’s journey.  
* Keep, edit, or rewrite its accumulated skills and flaws.  
* Revise the existing quest if it continues in a new form, or write a new one.

This is not merely an experience reward. Completing the quest changes how the character is described and mechanically represented.

### **Forsaking a quest**

After the third Forsake:

* Retitle the Motif to represent the hero’s new identity.  
* Remove the Motif’s previous skills and flaws.  
* Choose a new starting skill and flaw.  
* Write a new quest.  
* Add Potential equal to all accumulated Acts and Forsakes.

Because reaching three in either direction resolves the quest, the transferred Potential cannot exceed five. This preserves progress from partially pursuing a quest before ultimately abandoning it.

### **Party advancement**

The party will likely use a simplified version of the Motif structure:

* One party Motif  
* One or more party skill and flaw tags  
* A Potential or Rapport track  
* One party quest or goal  
* Party Improvements

When the party reaches five Rapport, it may be able to:

* Add a party skill.  
* Add or remove a party flaw.  
* Gain a Party Improvement.

Party tags should answer, “What sort of group are we?” Examples might describe the party as researchers, wilderness experts, renowned heroes, or well-connected travelers.

Party Improvements may include:

* Camp facilities  
* Libraries or shrines  
* Potion makers  
* Magical campfires  
* Storage  
* Security  
* Communication tools  
* Improved Load  
* Couriers and supply networks  
* NPC allies  
* Crafting and preservation benefits

### **Bond advancement**

**Kin** has been removed as a term. The system will now consistently use **Bond**.

Bond advancement should share the same overall vocabulary as hero and party advancement but remain lighter:

* Bond identity or Motif  
* Bond skills  
* Bond flaws  
* Bond or Potential track  
* Bond Improvements

Bonds probably will not have their own quests, Acts, or Forsakes. Their Improvements may work through tiers or a template rather than full advancement trees.

The exact way heroes earn Bond still needs to be redesigned. The likely direction is to reward meaningful relationship scenes, protection, vulnerability, and interpersonal conflict without interrupting those scenes.

### **Open-ended character options**

Every predefined option should remain replaceable by player input.

The lists of Motifs, skills, flaws, and Improvements are intended to provide:

* Inspiration  
* Examples  
* Onboarding  
* A shared sense of genre

They should not constrain what experienced players can create.

Chris’s initial visual feedback was positive:

# **Meeting summary — September 3, 2026**

The meeting centered on a proposed redesign of damage, resistance, and recovery. Instead of asking one status system to handle every kind of consequence, the new approach separates **short-term Strain, lasting Statuses, emotional Conditions, and temporary Boons and Banes**.

**This is an experimental proposal, not an adopted replacement.** Ryan wants to compare it with the existing system before committing; Mike sees a promising improvement in how clearly it distinguishes different kinds of stakes.

## **The heart of the game**

### **Consequences should become part of the story**

The central concern was that a character-driven game should not ultimately reduce its heroes to bags of hit points—or piles of numerical statuses that cancel each other out.

The proposed system makes taking harm a meaningful decision:

* Can I absorb this pressure and keep going?  
* Should I risk a resistance roll?  
* Am I willing to accept a lasting injury to remain in the fight?  
* When should I withdraw, surrender, or make a last stand?

A lasting injury becomes something that happened to the hero, with a fictional origin and continuing consequences. It is not simply another number to subtract.

### **Players choose the price of staying in the action**

The most distinctive idea is that **players choose whether to accept a lasting Status to reduce incoming Strain**.

The GM presents the danger. The player decides how their hero bears it.

This supports the game’s emphasis on dramatic choices while reducing the GM’s burden of determining every injury’s severity and description. It also gives heroes room to attempt something bold, absorb immediate pressure, and then decide whether continuing is worth a more enduring cost.

### **Different consequences need different lifespans**

The redesign separates:

* Pressure that matters **right now**  
* Injuries that remain **after the scene**  
* Emotional distress expressed through Conditions  
* Advantages and disadvantages that last only while the fiction supports them

That distinction should make consequences easier to understand and track while preserving their narrative meaning.

### **Clear stakes do not remove dramatic uncertainty**

The group also explored making defeat consequences explicit before combat.

Players might know that enemies intend to kill, capture, enslave, or drive them away. That clarity would allow informed decisions about surrender, resistance, and sacrifice without requiring the characters themselves to possess complete knowledge.

The desired principle is: **the table can understand the stakes while the characters still inhabit uncertainty.**

## **Proposed rules changes**

### **Conditions remain**

The proposal retains Conditions as the principal expression of emotional and mental distress.

Both designers like Conditions, but acknowledged that their positive experiences with them may create a bias. They should remain because they serve this game, not simply because they worked well in previous campaigns.

The precise boundary between Conditions and lasting mental or social Statuses still needs clarification.

### **Strain: immediate pressure**

Strain represents short-term damage, stress, dwindling luck, or expenditure of magical resources.

The current prototype uses **five numbered boxes**:

* Mark only one box per incoming effect.  
* Mark the box matching the final Strain value.  
* If that box is already occupied, mark the next available higher box.  
* Taking two Strain means marking box 2—not boxes 1 and 2\.  
* Strain clears at the end of the scene or combat in which it was incurred.

This makes Strain both a buffer against danger and a possible resource cost for moves.

The track’s final size is undecided. Alternatives discussed included:

* Five fixed boxes  
* A capacity derived from a Virtue  
* Separate physical and mental/magical tracks

### **Resisting Strain**

The draft offers two ways to reduce incoming Strain.

**Option 1: Roll to resist**

Roll with the relevant Virtue and applicable modifiers:

* **7–9:** Reduce incoming Strain by the value of the Virtue used.  
* **10+:** Reduce it by that value plus one.  
* **Miss:** Take the full incoming Strain.

For example, resisting with a Virtue of 2 would prevent two Strain on a mixed success or three on a full success.

**Option 2: Accept a lasting Status**

Instead of rolling, take a Status that absorbs a fixed amount of Strain.

After resistance, mark the remaining Strain using the numbered-box procedure.

A major balance concern is ensuring that rolling does not become so effective that accepting a Status is never worthwhile. A resource cost or other restriction on repeated resistance was suggested, but not decided.

### **Statuses: lasting consequences**

Statuses would no longer be a collection of independent ranked tracks. Instead, they occupy a limited set of slots:

| Severity | Available slots | Strain prevented | Penalty when relevant |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Minor | 3 | 2 | −1 to the roll |
| Major | 2 | 4 | Disadvantage |
| Severe | 1 | 6 | Roll one d6 instead of two |

Each Status receives a fictional description appropriate to what happened.

Examples discussed included:

* **Minor:** Twisted ankle, ringing ears, bruised ribs  
* **Major:** Deep wound, frozen arm, concussion  
* **Severe:** Crushed leg, life-threatening injury, shattered psyche

The penalty applies only when the Status is relevant to the action.

This structure creates escalating consequences while placing a clear limit on how many lasting effects a character can carry.

### **What happens when Strain cannot be marked?**

This remains unresolved.

The working assumption used during the discussion was that the hero becomes **Subdued** in the scene when no eligible Strain box remains. Other possibilities included forced Statuses or a threshold that creates one final moment before defeat.

The group wants to reconnect this with the existing **Last Stand** concept.

Possible outcomes discussed—not adopted—include:

* A final heroic action  
* Capture or incapacitation  
* Withdrawal or retirement  
* A lasting consequence  
* Other player-facing choices about how the hero leaves the scene

Capture could also allow someone to temporarily play another character while the party undertakes a rescue.

### **Surrender and defeat stakes**

A voluntary surrender or concession option was explored as an alternative to risking further harm.

That raises an important question: can the player choose capture when the enemy intends to kill?

One possible answer is to establish the enemy’s intent and the encounter’s defeat consequences explicitly. The current combat framework apparently provides a failure condition followed by a GM hard move, but may benefit from clearer guidance about what losing means.

No final surrender or capture rule was established.

### **Recuperate and healing**

The proposed **Recuperate** move uses Strain to limit repeated healing.

To Recuperate:

1. Spend an action if in combat.  
2. Take two Strain.  
3. Immediately remove one Minor Status.  
4. Roll with Mettle to advance a five-segment healing track.

Healing progress would be:

* **Miss:** Mark one segment.  
* **7–9:** Mark two.  
* **10+:** Mark three.

When the healing track fills:

* Reduce every remaining Status by one severity rank.  
* Rename the Statuses to reflect their improved state.  
* Clear the healing track.  
* Carry excess healing progress into the newly cleared track.

This creates a recovery process that can improve several injuries together while using immediate pressure to constrain healing.

The anti-spam mechanism still needs testing, especially outside combat and around scene boundaries.

### **Boons and Banes**

Boons and Banes replace temporary numerical statuses with unranked situational tags.

Examples include:

* **Boons:** Alert, blessed, trusted, high ground, shielded  
* **Banes:** Intoxicated, indebted, surprised, exposed, cursed

For a particular roll:

* More relevant Boons than Banes grants advantage.  
* More relevant Banes than Boons imposes disadvantage.  
* An equal number provides neither through this comparison.

They disappear when the supporting situation is no longer true.

The intent is to preserve meaningful fictional advantages without accumulating a large stack of numerical modifiers.

* Establish how clearly encounter failure consequences should be communicated.  
* Test healing across both combat and noncombat scenes.  
* Check interactions among skills, flaws, Conditions, Statuses, Boons, Banes, and other modifiers.

## **Principal unresolved questions**

* Is the new system better overall, or does it mainly expose distinctions the existing system needs?  
* Where exactly is the boundary between Conditions and mental or social Statuses?  
* Does exceeding Strain capacity immediately Subdue a hero, impose a Status, or create a final threshold?  
* What makes choosing a Status worthwhile compared with repeatedly rolling to resist?  
* Can players reliably choose capture or surrender, and when is that option unavailable?  
* What prevents repeatedly ending scenes to clear Strain and continue healing?  
* How do multiple Status penalties interact with advantage, disadvantage, and severe one-die rolls?  
* What happens when incoming Strain exceeds six, or the desired Status tier has no free slot?  
* How should failure consequences connect to combat goals?

One rules-writing inconsistency also needs attention: Strain is initially described as numbered slots, but the healing discussion sometimes treats it as an accumulating total. Repeated two-Strain costs would occupy progressively higher available slots under the opening rule—not simply produce a total of four, then six. The examples need to use one consistent procedure.

# **Meeting summary — September 15, 2026**

This meeting marked the transition from designing separate systems to reviewing something close to a complete playable ruleset. Ryan completed a consistency pass, formalized the universal Hero Roll, finished the first version of party creation and advancement, and substantially expanded combat and enemy design.

The two largest unfinished systems are now **Bonds** and **Improvements**, with GM hard/soft moves also identified as missing guidance.

## **The heart of the game**

### **The party is mechanically a character**

The strongest identity development is that the party now has its own:

* Motif  
* Skills and flaws  
* Quest  
* Advancement resource  
* Improvements

The party is not merely a collection of individual sheets. It has an identity and an arc.

This directly supports the game’s found-family premise: disparate heroes establish why they travel together, discover what they are good and bad at as a group, pursue a shared quest, and become more capable through cooperation and conflict.

### **Combat progresses the story even on bad rolls**

Outside combat, a miss means the hero does not achieve the intended outcome and the situation changes.

Combat uses a different scale because each roll represents a smaller moment. A low combat roll still produces limited impact rather than accomplishing nothing. The GM gains Misfortune, so the roll still creates consequences.

This avoids turns where a player waits for their chance to act, misses, and contributes nothing.

### **Tactical play emerges from the fiction**

Enemies have readable strengths and weaknesses rather than relying entirely on opaque numerical defenses.

Trying to overpower a mighty ogre creates a Bane. Luring an unintelligent ogre into a trap creates a Boon. Players do not need to know a hidden formula to understand that one approach is difficult and another exploits a weakness.

This reinforces the game’s central niche:

> **Tactical, but not crunchy; narrative, but still mechanically consequential.**

### **Stakes should be explicit**

The combat goal establishes what the heroes are trying to accomplish. The meeting identified that the game also needs to state what they are risking.

If the heroes declare that they intend to rescue a prisoner or defend a castle, the GM may state the consequences of failure before the fight begins. This lets everyone make informed dramatic decisions without requiring the characters themselves to possess complete knowledge.

## **Key decisions and working rules**

### **Full rules consistency pass**

Ryan reviewed the rules for:

* Contradictory procedures  
* Repeated explanations  
* Inconsistent terminology  
* Rules that should refer back to a shared definition  
* Places where Boons and Banes could replace separate modifiers

Many sections received editorial changes even where their mechanics remained the same.

### **The Hero Roll**

The standard roll now has an explicit name and procedure.

When a hero uses a move:

1. Roll 2d6.  
2. Add the relevant Virtue.  
3. Add \+1 for one relevant skill tag.  
4. Apply −1 for each relevant flaw tag.  
5. Optionally Push Yourself once by marking a Condition and adding \+1 for a second relevant skill.  
6. Cap the final modifier between −3 and \+3.  
7. Apply Advantage or Disadvantage from Boons, Banes, and other rules.

This provides one shared reference whenever another rule says to make a Hero Roll.

### **Out-of-combat results**

Outside combat:

* **10+:** Achieve the intended result, sometimes with an additional benefit.  
* **7–9:** Achieve it with a cost or complication.  
* **6−:** Do not achieve it; the situation changes, usually for the worse.

On a miss:

* The GM gains one Misfortune.  
* The GM may immediately make a hard move.  
* The hero marks Potential on one Motif.

The exact broader uses of Misfortune still need to be documented.

### **Combat results**

Combat rolls always create some impact:

* **10+:** Maximum impact  
* **7–9:** Moderate impact  
* **6−:** Limited impact, and the GM gains Misfortune

Combat receives this separate result structure because it examines the fiction in smaller increments than ordinary play.

### **Hero Improvements**

Characters will select two Hero Improvements during creation:

* Two starting Improvements from different trees, or  
* A starting Improvement and a connected Improvement beneath it

The actual Improvements and tree structure remain unwritten.

The central design requirement is that Improvements feel guided but flexible. It is still undecided whether:

* Narrative and combat Improvements share trees  
* Each tree mixes both types  
* They belong to separate categories

For an MVP, blank or freeform Improvements may be sufficient while the rest of the game is tested.

## **Party creation**

### **Party Motifs**

After creating their heroes, the players create the party together.

The Party Motif defines why these disparate individuals travel together. Thirteen example Motifs currently cover groups such as:

* The Banished  
* The Covenant  
* The Fellowship  
* Seekers  
* The Vengeful  
* The Entangled

Players can rename an example or create their own.

The Party Motif holds the group’s:

* Two skill tags  
* Two flaw tags  
* Party Quest  
* Rapport  
* Eventual Improvements

### **Limited party tags**

Party skills and flaws are shared resources:

* A hero may invoke a relevant party skill for \+1 without Pushing Themself.  
* The GM may invoke a relevant party flaw.  
* Each party tag can be used for only one Hero Roll before being expended.  
* Expended tags refresh when the group makes camp.

This gives players a meaningful decision about when to call upon the party’s shared strengths. It also prevents party tags from becoming permanent bonuses on every roll.

Using either a party skill or party flaw marks Rapport.

### **Party Quests**

The Party Quest is the group’s shared reason for traveling as one. It should lead the party into adventures while turning strangers and acquaintances into lifelong friends.

Four starting forms were proposed:

* **Shared Vision:** A way the party wants to change the world  
* **Shared Covenant:** A vow or ideology the group will uphold  
* **Shared Shield:** A people, place, or society the group will protect  
* **Shared Expedition:** A mystery, frontier, or legendary truth the group seeks

The rules recommend connecting the Party Quest to one or more individual Hero Quests. This gives the GM ready-made material and aligns player and campaign interests without requiring every hero to possess identical motives.

### **Party advancement**

The party marks Rapport when:

* A party skill is invoked  
* A party flaw is invoked  
* The Party Quest reaches an Act Break or Forsake  
* The heroes enter combat united behind the same goal  
* End-of-session reflection awards Rapport

At five Rapport, the party can advance when making camp by choosing one:

* Add a party skill tag  
* Add or remove a party flaw tag  
* Gain a Party Improvement

Party Improvements should initially be simpler than Hero Improvement trees. A flat list with repeatable Improvements may be sufficient.

### **Camp assets**

Camp assets may be folded into Party Improvements rather than remaining a separate subsystem.

Camp-related Improvements could represent cooking, storage, crafting, communication, allies, security, or other shared resources. Their associated tags should remain useful outside camp when the fiction supports them.

## **End-of-session procedure**

At the end of a session, the group answers:

* Did we uncover something new about the world?  
* Did we save someone or something from certain doom?  
* Did we learn something new about each other or cooperate especially well?  
* Did we defeat a major villain or important enemy?  
* Did we make meaningful progress toward our Party Quest?

If one or two answers are yes, mark one Rapport. If three or more are yes, mark two.

Each player then chooses one:

* **Grow closer:** Explain how you grew closer to another hero and mark Bond.  
* **Grow into your changes:** Rewrite one skill or flaw tag to better reflect the hero now.  
* **Grow toward your goal:** Mark Potential on a Motif whose quest advanced.

Party Quests use the same three escalating Act Breaks and three Forsakes as Hero Quests.

## **Conditions, Boons, and Banes**

Conditions now consistently create associated Banes instead of imposing fixed −2 penalties.

If a Condition is relevant to a roll, it contributes to the Boon/Bane comparison:

* More relevant Boons grants Advantage.  
* More relevant Banes imposes Disadvantage.

Marking the final available Condition causes the hero to **Crumble**:

* The hero must leave the scene.  
* In combat, the hero can only flee or avoid the action.  
* The hero may choose to become unconscious.  
* The Crumble procedure includes clearing a Condition and discussing how the hero eventually returns.

Condition timing is now consistent:

* Moves and magical effects that say to mark or clear a Condition do so immediately.  
* Fictional actions taken to clear a Condition resolve at the end of the scene.

## **Strain and resistance**

Resistance no longer depends on the value of the Virtue used. That version proved too swingy.

When an attack deals Strain, the GM offers one or two Virtues that could reasonably resist it. The player chooses one and describes the defense.

The hero may instead propose another Virtue if the fiction supports it. The GM may attach a Bane if that alternative is particularly difficult.

Resistance now reduces Strain by a fixed amount:

* **10+:** Reduce incoming Strain by 2\.  
* **7–9:** Reduce it by 1\.  
* **6−:** Take the full amount.

The hero may instead accept one or more Statuses. After resisting, the hero can spend an Action Point and use suitable Armor to negate the remaining Strain.

Before the player chooses, the GM should disclose:

* Incoming Strain  
* Any incoming Condition  
* Suggested resistance Virtues  
* Additional effects

### **Strain scale**

The current GM guidance is:

* **1:** Nuisance  
* **2:** Light pressure  
* **3:** Standard dangerous attack  
* **4:** Heavy elite or legendary attack  
* **5:** Dire attack that should usually be telegraphed  
* **6:** Exceptionally dangerous and rare

These values require playtesting because Strain uses numbered escalating boxes rather than ordinary hit points.

## **Adventure and basic moves**

### **Work Together**

A new Adventure Move resolves actions performed by the group as one.

When multiple heroes cooperate:

* One hero makes the Hero Roll.  
* Each participating hero may contribute one relevant skill tag.  
* Relevant party tags may also apply.  
* The result affects the entire group.

This prevents situations such as an entire party rolling separately to sneak into the same building.

Basic Moves represent recurring individual actions. Adventure Moves address larger group or adventure procedures such as making camp or working together. Improvements may modify either category but remain their own type.

### **Aid overlaps party skills**

The existing Aid move lets someone spend Rapport to add \+1 to another hero’s roll. Invoking a party skill now provides a very similar benefit.

The meeting established that these systems overlap too heavily. Aid must be:

* Removed  
* Combined with party tags  
* Or redesigned to do something meaningfully different

The group has not yet selected the final solution.

Likewise, whether selfish actions reduce Rapport or interfere with Bond remains unresolved. The designers want interpersonal harm to matter but need to place it in the correct relationship system.

## **Combat revisions**

### **Combat goal and failure consequence**

Combat already begins by stating a goal. The rules should probably also establish the consequences of failing to reach that goal.

This could turn combat into an explicit wager:

* The heroes state what they hope to accomplish.  
* The GM states what will happen if they fail.  
* Both sides enter the fight knowing what is at stake.

This remains a proposed addition.

### **Action Points and Prepare**

Rounds, turns, and Action Points were clarified.

A new **Prepare** action allows a hero to increase their maximum Action Points to four on their next turn. It currently cannot stack.

More elaborate charging or exhaustion mechanics may eventually appear on specific abilities rather than in the universal action.

### **Repeated attacks**

Repeated Strain-inflicting moves become increasingly difficult during the same turn:

* First attack: Resolve normally  
* Second attack: Worsen the roll by one Advantage/Disadvantage step  
* Third and subsequent attacks: Worsen it further, to a maximum of Double Disadvantage

Double Disadvantage means rolling four dice and keeping the lowest two.

Existing Advantage can offset these penalties. The goal is to stop “attack every time” from becoming the universally optimal turn.

### **Guard and Pierce**

Enemies may possess **Guard**, which reduces incoming Strain by a fixed amount.

Guard cannot reduce a hero’s attack below one Strain.

**Pierce** allows an attack to ignore Guard.

Heroes use Armor rather than Guard.

### **Immobilized**

Most temporary effects can be represented as Boons and Banes. The one necessary special case identified was **Immobilized**.

Immobilized is a special Bane covering grappling, paralysis, magical restraint, petrification, and other effects that prevent voluntary movement. While Immobilized, the creature cannot move.

## **Enemy design**

### **Enemy profiles**

Four profiles currently define the number of heroes an enemy is expected to challenge:

| Profile | Draft threat value | Intended opposition |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Minion | ½ | Two minions per hero |
| Standard | 1 | One enemy per hero |
| Elite | 2 | One enemy against two heroes |
| Legendary | 4 | One enemy against an entire four-hero party |

These values are early encounter-building guidance, not finalized balance.

Standard enemies currently:

* Move six spaces  
* Have range one  
* Have no Guard by default  
* Take one movement and one action  
* May exchange either for the other  
* May trade an attack to clear a Condition immediately

### **Relative threat levels**

Heroes and enemies do not possess conventional levels. Threat therefore describes how dangerous an enemy is to the current heroes.

The same ogre might be a major danger during the party’s first adventure and a routine obstacle later. The GM can adjust Strain, Status capacity, Conditions, attacks, and other dials without needing a separate canonical stat block for every level.

Total party Improvements may eventually provide a reference for expected threat, but the game’s advancement is largely horizontal, complicating a direct formula.

### **Enemy Virtues**

Enemy Virtues express strengths, weaknesses, and expected behavior.

A Virtue may have:

* No mark: Neutral  
* One or more pluses: Strong  
* One or more minuses: Weak

When a hero directly opposes a strong Virtue, each plus creates a Bane. When a hero exploits a weak Virtue, each minus creates a Boon.

Conditions can degrade these Virtues:

* Strong becomes neutral.  
* Neutral becomes weak.

This makes tactical information readable through the fiction rather than hidden in abstract defenses.

### **Resolving enemy attacks**

The GM:

1. Selects an attack.  
2. Describes what the enemy attempts.  
3. States incoming Strain, Conditions, suggested Virtues, and additional effects.  
4. Allows the hero to choose how to defend.  
5. Resolves resistance, accepted Statuses, and Armor.

This procedure is meant to eliminate “gotcha” interactions by telling the player what the game already knows before they commit.

### **Legendary enemies**

Legendary enemies act after every hero’s turn and are structured around three phases:

1. Opening  
2. Bloodied  
3. Last Stand

They unlock new features as their phases change. Transitions occur through their Strain mechanics and should be clearly telegraphed.

## **Clocks and threats**

The clock rules and clock subtypes were completed or substantially refined.

**Threat Clocks** received particular attention. They represent antagonists or dangers that arise organically during play rather than belonging to the original adventure. They sit between:

* A normal challenge clock  
* The adventure’s primary Countdown

This gives the GM a lightweight way to preserve consequences and developing opposition created through improvisation.
